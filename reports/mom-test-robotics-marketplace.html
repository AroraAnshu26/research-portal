The Mom Test, pointed at a startup-to-startup robotics parts exchange

A question set and interview framework built for one specific job: in the days before the YC deadline, turn an unlimited contact list into evidence. Not encouragement. The design constraint is that you are testing three things at once — whether the pain is real, which side of the market is the hard side, and whether people pay for speed or for selection — so these questions are built to discriminate between answers, not merely to confirm that sourcing is annoying.

Source: Rob Fitzpatrick, The Mom Test (2013) — three rules, three kinds of bad data, commitment currencies. Applied to the landscape in the robotics-marketplace scan on this portal.

What these two weeks have to settle

You said the hard side and the fulfilment model are both open questions you want tested. That is the single most important input to this design. A generic "is this a problem?" interview will not separate them, because almost everyone will agree sourcing is painful — and agreement is the cheapest thing you can collect. Every question set below is built to force a choice.

Decision 1 · Problem
Is the pain real, recent and expensive?
Not "is it annoying". Does it have a date, a duration and a number attached? A problem nobody has spent money or built a workaround for is a preference, not a problem.
Decision 2 · Hard side
Buyers or sellers — who is actually desperate?
You build the side that is not desperate and recruit the side that is. Ask both populations the same urgency probe and compare. Most founders assume supply is hard and discover demand is harder, or the reverse — but they assume rather than measure.
Decision 3 · Model
Do they pay for speed, or for selection?
This decides whether you hold inventory or stay asset-light, and it is settled by one question about the last purchase (see the spine, move 5). Speed buyers took a worse part because it was in stock. Selection buyers waited weeks because nothing matched spec. They are different businesses.
The trap specific to your situation

You have unlimited contacts and very little time. That combination pushes hard toward volume of friendly conversations, which is exactly the failure mode the book is about. Twenty warm chats that all go well produce less than six conversations that each end in a commitment or a clear refusal. Pick the harder meetings.

The spine: seven moves, same every meeting

Run this with every segment. The segment-specific pages only change the vocabulary and moves 5 and 6. Budget 30–40 minutes. Do not deviate into pitching even if invited.

  1. Open on their world, not your idea
    No preamble about what you are building. One line of framing and then straight into their work.
    • "I'm trying to understand how hardware teams actually get parts. Can you tell me what you're building right now and where it is?"
    • "What's blocking you this week?" Unprompted mention of sourcing here is the strongest possible signal. Note whether it came up on its own.
  2. Anchor to the last concrete event
    This is the hinge of the entire interview. Everything downstream hangs off a specific, dated episode.
    • "Walk me through the last time you needed a part you didn't already have."
    • "When was that? What was the part?"
    • "What did you do first?" Then just follow it. Do not interrupt to sympathise.
  3. Timeline it — get the duration
    You are converting a feeling into a number. Push gently for dates until the story has edges.
    • "How long between realising you needed it and having it in your hand?"
    • "How much of that was finding it versus waiting for it?" This split matters enormously — it is discovery pain versus logistics pain, and they imply different companies.
    • "What else stopped while you waited? Was anyone sitting idle?"
  4. Money — who paid, and what did the delay cost
    Ask plainly. Hardware people are usually comfortable with these numbers.
    • "What did the part cost? And what did the week cost you?"
    • "Whose budget did it come out of? Do you need approval, and from whom?" Identifies the real buyer, who is often not your contact.
    • "Did you pay anything to make it faster — expedite, air freight, a premium distributor?" A yes here is hard evidence for the speed model.
  5. The discriminator — speed or selection
    Two questions, asked exactly like this. They settle Decision 3 better than any amount of discussion.
    • "Did you end up with the part you wanted, or the part you could get fastest?" "The one I could get" → speed buyer → argues for holding inventory.
    • "Have you ever waited weeks because nothing else matched the spec?" Yes → selection buyer → argues for asset-light breadth of catalogue.
    • "When you compromised, what did it cost you later?" Rework, a redesign, a failed test — this is where the real number usually hides.
  6. Workarounds — what have they already built or bought
    Existing spend and existing hacks are the highest-quality evidence available. Someone who built a spreadsheet has told you the problem is real without being asked.
    • "How do you keep track of what you've ordered and from whom today?"
    • "Has anyone on the team built something internal for this — a sheet, a script, a vendor list?"
    • "Have you paid for any tool or service to help here? What happened to it?"
    • "Who else have you tried? What made you stop using them?" Direct competitive intelligence, volunteered.
  7. Close on a commitment, or learn that there isn't one
    Never end on "this was great, I'll keep you posted." Make a specific ask and record what they actually said. A refusal is a result.
    • "What's the next part you'll need to source? When?"
    • "If I found that at spec, in stock, delivered by [date] — would you buy it from me?" Then stop talking.
    • "Who are your two best suppliers? Would you introduce me?"
    • "Who else should I be speaking to who has this problem worse than you?"
If they ask what you're building

Deflect once: "I'll tell you at the end — I don't want to bias what you tell me." They will respect it. When you do explain at the end, frame it to invite attack: "The thing I'm least sure about is whether anyone would trust an unknown vendor for a part their robot depends on. Tell me why that fails." That phrasing gets you criticism, which is data. "Sounds great" is not.

The questions you will be tempted to ask, and what to ask instead

These are the specific traps for this idea. The left column is what a founder with a marketplace thesis and a deadline naturally says. The right column is the version that produces evidence.

Don't ask
"Would you use a marketplace where startups buy parts from other startups?"
Hypothetical + your idea + a yes costs them nothing.
Ask
"Where did the last five parts on your BOM actually come from? Walk me down the list."
Reveals the real supplier mix, including how much is already small-vendor.
Don't ask
"Do you have trouble sourcing actuators?"
Leading. Everyone says yes. Tells you nothing about magnitude.
Ask
"What was the longest you've been blocked waiting on a component this year? What was it?"
Forces a specific worst case with a duration you can compare across interviews.
Don't ask
"Would you pay a 10% fee for faster sourcing?"
Pricing hypotheticals are the purest form of fluff.
Ask
"Have you ever paid a premium to get a part faster? What was the part, and how much over list?"
Past behaviour. If they have expedited before, they will again.
Don't ask
"Would you buy from a vendor you'd never heard of?"
Invites the socially correct answer, which is no.
Ask
"Tell me about the last time you bought from a supplier you didn't know. How did you decide it was safe?"
Gives you the actual trust mechanism — which is the thing you'd have to build.
Don't ask
"Would you list your parts on a platform like this?" (to a vendor)
Sellers say yes to any free distribution and then never upload a SKU.
Ask
"How did your last five customers find you? What did you spend to get them?"
If their CAC is near zero they do not need you; if it's painful, you have a wedge.
Don't ask
"How much would you pay for access to simulation environments?"
Asks them to price something that does not exist, in a category they may not buy in at all.
Ask
"What did you use to test the last policy before it touched a real robot? Did that cost anything?"
Finds out whether sim is a purchased line item or something they build themselves — which decides whether the expansion is real.
Don't ask
"Is China sourcing a problem for you?"
Invites geopolitics commentary rather than their operational reality.
Ask
"What's on your BOM that you can only get from one place? What happens if that lead time doubles?"
Surfaces genuine single-source exposure, which is where urgency and budget actually live.
Don't ask
"Do you think other startups would find this useful?"
Asks them to speculate about other people, the least reliable data of all.
Ask
"Who do you know who has this worse than you? Can you introduce me?"
Converts the same instinct into a reputation commitment and a lead.

Five populations, and they are not all customers

Read this before the rest of the page

You listed GSB supply-chain people and PhD students alongside robot founders. Treat them completely differently. A GSB student who knows Chinese supply chains is an expert witness, not a customer — asking them validation questions produces confident, fluent, worthless answers. PhD students are buyers, but with near-zero budget authority and tiny volumes: excellent for proving the problem exists and frequent, misleading for proving anyone will pay. Mixing these three kinds of evidence into one pile is the most likely way this fortnight goes wrong.

A · Robotics founders building and deploying hardware

Core customer
Your primary buyer, and the only segment that can give you all three decisions at once. Over-weight these: aim for half your meetings here.
After the spine, add
  • "How many distinct suppliers are on your current BOM? Who owns that relationship — you, or someone else?"
  • "What's the difference between how you sourced at prototype stage and how you source now?" Finds the stage at which your product would be adopted — and whether they grow out of you.
  • "When you need a part fast, who do you call or message first?" Often a person, not a site. That person is your real competitor.
  • "Have you ever designed around a part because you couldn't get the one you wanted?"
  • "What fraction of your parts come from companies smaller than yours?" Directly tests your startup-to-startup premise.
Commitment ask
  • "What's the next part you need? Let me try to source it this week — if I hit your spec and date, will you buy?"

B · Component vendors and small hardware suppliers

The other side — interview these
You did not list these, and that is the biggest gap in your plan. You cannot test "which side is the hard side" by only talking to one side. Find at least five sellers — small actuator, gripper, sensor and machining shops, including the YC ones on the marketplace scan.
Questions
  • "How did your last five customers find you?"
  • "What did you spend to get them — time, ads, conferences, intros?"
  • "How much inbound do you get that you can't serve, and why can't you serve it?" Wasted inbound is the clearest reason a seller joins a marketplace.
  • "What's your lead time right now, honestly, versus what your site says?"
  • "Have you ever let a third party quote or sell on your behalf? What happened?"
  • "Would you give someone else your stock levels? What would stop you?" This is the operational blocker for any real-time catalogue, and it is usually the answer that kills naive marketplace plans.
Commitment ask
  • "Can you send me your current price and lead-time list for your top ten SKUs this week?" If they will not send a price list, they will not join a marketplace.

C · Simulation and robot-software founders

Adjacent — tests the expansion, not the launch
You said physical parts only at launch, so these conversations are not about your first product. Their job is to tell you whether the sim half is a real second act or a slide. Hold them to that and do not let them redesign your roadmap.
Questions
  • "Who pays you today, and what exactly are they buying — a licence, a service, a dataset?"
  • "How do customers find you now?"
  • "When a robotics team comes to you, what have they usually already tried?"
  • "Do your customers ever ask you to recommend hardware? What do you tell them?" If sim vendors are already informally routing hardware decisions, that is a real distribution insight.
  • "Is your environment tied to specific hardware? How much work is a new arm?" Tests whether a combined hardware-plus-sim listing is even coherent.
Commitment ask
  • "Would you co-list with a hardware vendor on a trial basis — your environment, their arm, one bundle?"

D · PhD students and lab buyers

Real pain Weak willingness to pay
Use them for frequency and friction data, which they have in abundance, and discount everything they say about price. University procurement is a distinct beast — purchase orders, approved-vendor lists, grant codes — and if you ever sell here, that machinery is the product, not the catalogue.
Questions
  • "How often do you order parts — weekly, monthly?"
  • "Walk me through what happens between you deciding you need something and it arriving. Who else touches it?" Expect a purchasing office. Count the steps.
  • "How long does the paperwork add on top of shipping?"
  • "Have you ever bought something with your own money to avoid the process?" A yes is a strong pain signal — and tells you the official channel is failing.
  • "Is your lab locked to approved vendors? Who gets a new supplier onto that list, and how long does it take?" This single answer tells you whether academia is reachable at all.
Commitment ask
  • "Can you forward me your last three purchase orders with the prices redacted?"

E · GSB and supply-chain experts (incl. China)

Not customers — expert witnesses
Do not ask these people whether your idea is good. They will give you a fluent, structured, plausible answer, and it will be worth nothing because they are not the ones with the problem. Ask them only for mechanism: how the trade actually works, where the margin sits, what will break at volume.
Questions — mechanism only
  • "If I wanted to buy 50 of one actuator from a Shenzhen supplier next month, what does that process actually look like end to end?"
  • "Where does the margin sit in that chain today, and who captures it?"
  • "What do incumbent distributors do that isn't obvious from outside?" The unglamorous answer — financing, credit terms, returns, customs — is usually the real moat and the real cost.
  • "What are the failure modes when a small intermediary tries this? Where do they typically die?"
  • "What's the minimum order reality? Would a supplier take a 5-unit order from an unknown startup at all?" This one question may invalidate or confirm the whole startup-to-startup premise.
  • "Who holds the working capital in that chain?" If the answer is 'the distributor', your asset-light model has a financing problem you have not costed.
Commitment ask
  • "Can you introduce me to one supplier and one buyer you'd trust?" Their network is the asset here, not their opinion.

The honesty check

If after fifteen interviews you have no disconfirming notes and every meeting felt good, the data is bad — not the idea validated. Fitzpatrick's whole point is that the pleasant conversation is the failure mode. Go back and re-read your notes for compliments you recorded as evidence.

Twelve days, worked backwards from the deadline

Dated from today, 21 October, against a deadline on or about 2 November — confirm the exact date before you use this schedule. The shape matters more than the dates: interview the two sides in parallel, force the discriminator early, and leave the last stretch for a real commitment rather than more conversations.

Day 1
Line up both sides before you talk to anyone
Book 8 buyers (segment A) and 5 sellers (segment B) in the same breath. The seller side is the one you will be tempted to skip and the one that answers Decision 2. Write down your three biggest unknowns and keep them visible in every call.
Days 2–4
First ten interviews, spine only
No pitching at all in this block, even at the end. Score each within ten minutes. By interview ten you should see whether "paid to go faster" or "waited on spec" is dominating.
Day 5
Read the pile and cut
Call Decision 3 provisionally. Drop the weakest segment entirely — probably D or E — and reallocate those slots to whichever side looked harder. This is the day the plan is supposed to change.
Days 6–9
Second ten, now with commitment asks
Same spine, but every meeting ends in a specific ask for time, reputation or money. Start running one real transaction by hand: take one buyer's actual next part and try to source it yourself. Doing it manually will teach you more than the next five interviews.
Days 10–11
Convert, don't collect
Go back to the three warmest and ask for the hard commitment — a deposit, an LOI, a named design partnership. Chase the manual transaction to completion if it is still open. One completed trade is worth more in the application than twenty interviews.
Day 12
Write it up
Three numbers (median days blocked, median cost of delay, how many already pay for a workaround), three verbatim quotes, the named commitments by currency, and the one thing you learned that you did not expect. That last item is what signals you actually ran the process.

Two things to settle before Day 1

  • Confirm the deadline. This schedule assumes ~2 November. If it is earlier, cut the second interview block, not the commitment days.
  • Find five sellers. Segment B is missing from your list and it is half your question. The robotics-marketplace scan on this portal has candidates: Hlabs, Open Actuation and Silica on actuators, Foundation Industries, Prototyping.io, twentyfour26 and Antropi on machined parts.
