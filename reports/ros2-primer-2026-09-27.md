## 1. The problem ROS exists to solve

Start with the naive design, because it is the one everybody reaches for first and it is instructive to watch it fail.

You have a wheeled robot. It has a spinning laser that measures distances, a gyroscope, two wheel motors, and a computer. You want it to drive around a room without hitting things and build a map as it goes. The obvious approach is one program: read the laser, read the gyro, do some arithmetic, write motor commands, loop forever.

This works, briefly, and then collapses for four reasons.

**Different parts of the job run at wildly different rates.** The motor controller needs to correct wheel velocity hundreds or thousands of times a second or the robot judders. The laser produces a scan maybe ten times a second. The mapping algorithm might take 200 milliseconds to fold one scan into a map. A decision like "go to the kitchen" happens once a minute. In one program with one loop, the slowest step sets the pace for everything, so your motor control now runs at the speed of your map optimiser. The robot becomes unstable for reasons that have nothing to do with control theory and everything to do with software structure.

**One crash kills everything.** The mapping code is the most complex and the most likely to fail. In a monolith, when it dies, your motor control dies with it, and the robot keeps rolling at its last commanded velocity into a wall.

**You cannot replace one piece.** Swap the laser for a different model and you are editing the same file that contains your control loop. Every improvement risks everything.

**You cannot see inside it.** When the robot behaves oddly you want to ask: what did the laser actually report at that moment, and what did the mapper believe? In a monolith those are local variables. They are gone.

The fix, arrived at independently many times across the history of robotics, is to split the system into many small programs, each doing one job, each running at its own rate, each able to crash and restart independently, communicating by passing messages. Your motor controller does not import your mapper; it subscribes to a stream of velocity commands and does not care who produces them.

Once you commit to that structure, three genuinely hard problems appear, and **solving these three problems is what ROS is**:

1. **Transport.** How does a message get from one process to another, efficiently, possibly across machines, without every program hard-coding the addresses of every other program?
2. **Naming and discovery.** How does a newly started program find the others, when the set of running programs changes constantly?
3. **Shared conventions.** If one program says the obstacle is at x = 2.3, relative to what? The laser? The robot centre? The room? And *when* was that true? Without agreed answers, every pair of programs invents its own and nothing composes.

ROS provides an answer to all three: a message-passing system, a discovery mechanism, and a large set of standard conventions covering message types, coordinate frames and time. Plus, in practice, a vast collection of existing programs that already speak those conventions, which is the real reason people use it. Someone has already written and debugged the laser driver, the SLAM algorithm, and the visualiser.

### ROS is not an operating system

The name is a historical embarrassment. ROS stands for Robot Operating System and it is not an operating system. It does not manage memory, schedule processes, or handle hardware interrupts. Linux does that. ROS is a **middleware and a toolkit**: a set of libraries you link against, a build system, a set of command-line tools, and a social convention about message formats. It runs *on* an operating system.

This matters for your setup, because it explains why you needed Ubuntu at all. ROS 2 Humble is distributed as Ubuntu 22.04 packages, so you needed a real Ubuntu 22.04 userspace, which is exactly what WSL and the Docker image provide.

## 2. ROS 1, ROS 2, and why your course pins Humble

ROS 1 launched in 2007 and had a structural flaw: a central process called the *master* held the registry of who publishes what. Every node registered with the master to find its peers. If the master died, no new connections could form. Fine in a lab, unacceptable in a product, and awkward for multi-robot systems.

ROS 2 is a rewrite, not an upgrade. Its central change is the removal of the master. ROS 2 delegates transport and discovery to **DDS** (Data Distribution Service), an industrial publish/subscribe standard from aerospace and defence. DDS participants find each other by announcing themselves on the local network and listening for others. There is no central authority to lose. The rewrite also brought real-time-friendly design, quality-of-service controls (section 4.2), and support for non-Linux targets.

The naming scheme trips up everyone. ROS 2 releases are alphabetical, named after turtles, roughly one per year, and each is pinned to one Ubuntu LTS:

| Distribution | Ubuntu | Released | Supported until |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foxy Fitzroy | 20.04 | 2020 | 2023 (ended) |
| **Humble Hawksbill** | **22.04** | **2022** | **May 2027** |
| Iron Irwini | 22.04 | 2023 | 2024 (ended) |
| Jazzy Jalisco | 24.04 | 2024 | 2029 |

**This single table explains the entire shape of your installation.** CS237A uses Humble, because Humble is the long-term-support release with the deepest package ecosystem. Humble binaries are built against Ubuntu 22.04 system libraries. Therefore you need Ubuntu 22.04 specifically, not 24.04 and not "some Linux". That is why the course doc had you install Ubuntu 22.04 in WSL, and why your container image begins with `FROM ros:humble`, which is itself built on Ubuntu 22.04. Every other decision in the setup is downstream of that one constraint.

It also explains why the Mac instructions in your course doc are several times longer than the Windows ones. A Mac cannot execute Ubuntu binaries at all, so Mac students must run a complete virtual machine with a full Ubuntu desktop inside it. On Windows, WSL 2 already provides a real Linux kernel and userspace, so you start one whole layer thinner.

## 3. The five core abstractions

Almost all of ROS reduces to five concepts. Learn these and the rest is detail.

### 3.1 Nodes

A **node** is one program doing one job. Conventionally a node is a single process, though several can be composed into one process for efficiency.

Your simulator started seven of them. From your own log:

```
[INFO] [robot_state_publisher-1]: process started with pid [44940]
[INFO] [gz sim-2]: process started with pid [44942]
[INFO] [create-3]: process started with pid [44944]
[INFO] [parameter_bridge-4]: process started with pid [44946]
[INFO] [static_transform_publisher-5]: process started with pid [44949]
[INFO] [static_transform_publisher-6]: process started with pid [44951]
[INFO] [async_slam_toolbox_node-7]: process started with pid [44953]
```

Seven processes, seven real operating-system PIDs, started by one command. Each could have been launched by hand in seven terminals; the launch file (section 8) simply does it for you. Nodes have names, which is how you address them. `ros2 node list` on your system showed `/robot_state_publisher`, `/ros_gz_bridge`, `/slam_toolbox` and the two transform publishers.

### 3.2 Topics: the one you will use constantly

A **topic** is a named, typed stream of messages. Nodes **publish** to it, other nodes **subscribe** to it. It is anonymous and many-to-many: a publisher does not know or care who is listening, and there may be zero listeners or ten.

A topic has a **name** (`/scan`) and a **type** (`sensor_msgs/msg/LaserScan`). The type is a schema, a fixed set of fields, and both sides must agree on it. Standard types are the heart of ROS interoperability: any SLAM package in the world can consume any laser driver in the world, because both speak `sensor_msgs/msg/LaserScan`.

Your run produced nineteen topics. The ones that matter:

| Topic | Type | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `/scan` | `sensor_msgs/LaserScan` | One revolution of laser distance readings |
| `/odom` | `nav_msgs/Odometry` | Position estimated by counting wheel rotations |
| `/imu` | `sensor_msgs/Imu` | Angular rates and accelerations |
| `/cmd_vel` | `geometry_msgs/Twist` | **Commanded** velocity. This is how you drive it |
| `/map` | `nav_msgs/OccupancyGrid` | The map SLAM has built so far |
| `/tf`, `/tf_static` | `tf2_msgs/TFMessage` | Coordinate frame relationships (section 5) |
| `/clock` | `rosgraph_msgs/Clock` | Simulation time (section 4.4) |
| `/joint_states` | `sensor_msgs/JointState` | Wheel joint positions |

There is an asymmetry here worth internalising. `/scan`, `/odom` and `/imu` are the robot telling you about the world. `/cmd_vel` is you telling the robot what to do. In this course your assignments will overwhelmingly consist of subscribing to the first group, computing something, and publishing to the second. In ROS terms, that is what a robot controller *is*.

You can inspect all of it from a terminal, which is the single most useful debugging habit to build:

```bash
ros2 topic list
```

```bash
ros2 topic echo /scan --once
```

```bash
ros2 topic hz /scan
```

The second prints one real message. The third reports the actual publication rate, which is invaluable when something feels sluggish.

### 3.3 Services: when you need an answer

Topics are fire-and-forget streams. Sometimes you want a question with a reply: reset the map, report your configuration. That is a **service**: a request/response pair, one-to-one, where the caller waits. Use it for occasional commands with a definite answer, never for streaming data.

### 3.4 Actions: for things that take time

Neither topics nor services fit "drive to the kitchen", which takes thirty seconds, should report progress, and might need cancelling. An **action** is the composite built for exactly this: a goal, periodic feedback, a final result, and a cancel facility. Under the hood it is services plus topics. Navigation goals are the canonical example, and you will meet them when the course reaches path planning.

### 3.5 Parameters

Configuration values a node reads at startup and sometimes at runtime: `max_speed`, `map_resolution`, `use_sim_time`. They are per-node and typed, and can be set from the command line, a YAML file, or a launch file. Your `slam_toolbox` reported `Node using stack size 40000000` and `Using solver plugin solver_plugins::CeresSolver`. Both are parameters the ASL launch file set for it.

### Choosing between them

| You want to | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Stream data continuously | **Topic** | Laser scans, velocity commands |
| Ask a question, get one answer | **Service** | "Clear the map" |
| Request a long task with progress | **Action** | "Navigate to this pose" |
| Configure a node | **Parameter** | `use_sim_time`, control gains |

The commonest beginner error is using a service where a topic belongs, which stalls your node while it waits. When in doubt, it is a topic.

## 4. The plumbing you will actually trip over

None of these four is conceptually deep, but between them they cause most of the lost afternoons.

### 4.1 Discovery, and the two variables in your config

Because ROS 2 has no master, nodes find each other by announcing themselves on the local network. Two consequences, both already configured on your machine:

**`ROS_DOMAIN_ID=42`** partitions the network. Nodes only see other nodes with the same domain ID, because the number maps to a distinct set of UDP ports. In a shared lab where twenty students run identical TurtleBot code on one network, identical domain IDs would mean your laser data arriving at your neighbour's mapper. Distinct IDs keep everyone isolated. Your devcontainer pins 42.

**`ROS_LOCALHOST_ONLY=1`** restricts all traffic to the loopback interface, so nothing leaves the machine. For simulation this is exactly right; it removes a whole class of confusing cross-talk. But note for later: when the course moves to *real* TurtleBots, talking to a robot over Wi-Fi requires turning this off. If your laptop mysteriously cannot see a physical robot, this variable is the first thing to check.

### 4.2 Quality of Service, and why messages get dropped

DDS lets every publisher and subscriber declare a **QoS profile**. The parts that matter:

- **Reliability.** `RELIABLE` retransmits until delivery is confirmed. `BEST_EFFORT` does not. Sensor streams usually want best-effort, because a laser scan from 200 ms ago is worthless and resending it wastes bandwidth.
- **History and depth.** `KEEP_LAST` with depth *n* buffers the most recent *n* messages and discards the rest. `KEEP_ALL` buffers everything.
- **Durability.** `TRANSIENT_LOCAL` lets a late-joining subscriber receive the last published value. This is why `/map` and `/tf_static` are still useful to a node that starts up minutes after them.

**A publisher and subscriber with incompatible QoS will not connect at all.** No error, no data, just silence. This is the most baffling failure mode in ROS 2. If `ros2 topic list` shows the topic, `ros2 topic hz` shows it publishing, and your subscriber still receives nothing, suspect QoS before anything else.

QoS is also the direct explanation for the one interesting line in your log:

```
slam_toolbox: Message Filter dropping message: frame 'asl_tb3/base_scan/velodyne'
at time 28.900 for reason 'discarding message because the queue is full'
```

The mechanism is this. `slam_toolbox` cannot process a laser scan the instant it arrives, because it first needs to know where the robot was *at that scan's timestamp*, which means it needs a coordinate transform for simulation time 28.900 exactly (section 5). So incoming scans go into a holding queue managed by a `tf2` **message filter**, which releases each scan only once the matching transform has arrived. If transforms run late, the queue fills and the oldest scan is dropped with exactly that message.

So the warning does not mean your laser is broken or SLAM is misconfigured. It means **the system briefly could not keep up in real time**, so transforms lagged behind scans. Your first run rendered Gazebo on the CPU and achieved roughly 0.58 times real-time, which produced precisely this. After the GPU was enabled, the same test dropped zero scans. One dropped scan is harmless. Hundreds would degrade your map, and that is your cue to reduce load.

### 4.3 Names, namespaces, and remapping

Topic and node names form a hierarchy like a filesystem path: `/robot1/scan`. A node can be launched into a **namespace**, which prefixes all of its names, and that is how you run two identical robots without collisions. Any name can also be **remapped** at launch, so the same driver publishes to `/scan` in one configuration and `/robot2/scan` in another with no code change. You will see this in the course launch files; it is worth knowing it exists before it confuses you.

### 4.4 Time, and why simulation has its own clock

This one is subtle and it matters enormously in simulation.

Nodes normally timestamp messages using the wall clock. But your simulation does **not** run at wall-clock speed: you measured 43 seconds of simulated time in 55 seconds of real time. If the mapper timestamps by wall clock while the laser timestamps by simulation clock, then every calculation involving time is wrong, and SLAM silently produces nonsense.

ROS solves this with a dedicated `/clock` topic. The simulator publishes authoritative simulation time, and every node started with the parameter `use_sim_time: true` reads time from `/clock` instead of the system clock. That is why `/clock` appears in your topic list, and why the bridge explicitly forwards it:

```
Creating GZ->ROS Bridge: [/clock (gz.msgs.Clock) -> /clock (rosgraph_msgs/msg/Clock)]
```

The practical rule: **in simulation every node needs `use_sim_time: true`, and a node that missed it will behave bizarrely.** The ASL launch files set it for you. When you start writing your own nodes for assignments and they misbehave around timing, this is an early suspect.

It also explains the timestamp in that dropped-message warning. `at time 28.900` is simulation time, 28.9 seconds into the simulated world, not a Unix timestamp.

## 5. Coordinate frames and tf2

This is the subsystem that confuses people longest, and it is worth slowing down for, because half of robotics debugging is frame debugging.

### The problem

Your laser reports "something solid, 2 metres away, 30 degrees off my centre". The laser is bolted to the top of the robot, a few centimetres up and forward of the robot's centre of rotation. The robot is somewhere in a room. Your planner wants to know where that obstacle is *in the room*.

Converting between those descriptions requires knowing every transform in the chain: laser to robot body, robot body to room. Some of those never change (the laser is bolted on). Others change constantly (the robot is driving). And any of them can be queried *at a particular past moment*, because the scan you are processing now was taken 100 ms ago, when the robot was somewhere slightly different.

### The solution

**tf2** is a library plus two topics, `/tf` and `/tf_static`, that together maintain a *tree* of coordinate frames over time. Any node can broadcast "frame B is at this position and orientation relative to frame A, as of this timestamp". Any node can then ask "what was the transform from frame X to frame Y at time T?" and tf2 will compose the chain and interpolate between the samples it has.

The rules: every frame has exactly one parent, there are no cycles, and one frame is the root. It is a tree, not a graph.

### Your robot's actual tree

Your log shows `robot_state_publisher` reading the robot description and reporting each link it found:

```
got segment base_footprint
got segment base_link
got segment base_scan
got segment caster_back_link
got segment imu_link
got segment left_wheel_link
got segment right_wheel_link
```

Combined with the two static publishers and the SLAM output, the tree looks like this:

```
map                              <- built by slam_toolbox, the room itself
 +-- odom                        <- the drifting wheel-odometry origin
      +-- base_footprint         <- robot position on the floor plane
           +-- base_link         <- robot body centre
                +-- base_scan    <- where the laser is mounted
                |    +-- asl_tb3/base_scan/velodyne   <- the sensor's own frame
                +-- imu_link
                |    +-- asl_tb3/imu_link/imu
                +-- left_wheel_link
                +-- right_wheel_link
                +-- caster_back_link
```

Some conventions worth knowing, because they are near-universal in ROS:

- **`base_link`** is the robot body. Everything bolted to the robot hangs off it.
- **`base_footprint`** is `base_link` projected straight down onto the floor. It exists so that 2D navigation code can ignore the robot's height.
- **`odom`** is a frame that is *continuous but drifts*. It is computed by integrating wheel rotations, so it never jumps, but error accumulates without bound as you drive.
- **`map`** is a frame that is *accurate but jumps*. SLAM corrects it whenever it recognises a place, so it does not drift, but the robot's position can jump discontinuously when a correction lands.

That last pair is the clever bit of the standard design, and it is worth pausing on. You genuinely need both properties, and no single frame can have both. A velocity controller needs smoothness and would be destabilised by jumps, so it works in `odom`. A route planner needs global accuracy and can tolerate jumps, so it works in `map`. The tree accommodates both by putting `map` above `odom` and letting SLAM adjust the `map` to `odom` transform to absorb all the correction.

### Static versus dynamic transforms

Your two `static_transform_publisher` nodes printed:

```
translation: ('0.000000', '0.000000', '0.000000')
rotation: ('0.000000', '0.000000', '0.000000', '1.000000')
from 'base_scan' to 'asl_tb3/base_scan/velodyne'
```

A **static** transform never changes, so it is published once on `/tf_static` with `TRANSIENT_LOCAL` durability and every future subscriber receives it on connection. A **dynamic** transform, like `odom` to `base_footprint`, is republished continuously on `/tf` as the robot moves.

This particular pair is a plumbing detail rather than physics: translation zero, rotation identity, meaning the two frames are in exactly the same place. It exists because Gazebo names the sensor frame `asl_tb3/base_scan/velodyne` in its own namespace while the robot description calls it `base_scan`. The static publisher glues the two naming schemes together. Without it, the tree would be split in two and SLAM would never find a path from the laser to the map.

The command you want when frames go wrong:

```bash
ros2 run tf2_tools view_frames
```

It writes a PDF of the whole tree. A **disconnected tree is the most common cause of a silently broken robot**, and it is instantly visible in that diagram.

## 6. Describing a robot: URDF, SDF, xacro

Several programs need to know the robot's shape: the visualiser to draw it, the physics engine to simulate it, `robot_state_publisher` to compute transforms, the planner to know how wide it is. So the shape lives in a file, in one of two XML formats.

**URDF** (Unified Robot Description Format) is the ROS one. It describes **links** (rigid bodies, each with a visual mesh, a collision shape, and inertial properties) and **joints** (connections between links, each with a type: fixed, revolute, continuous, prismatic, floating). A URDF is a tree, which is where your tf tree comes from.

**SDF** (Simulation Description Format) is Gazebo's, and it is strictly more expressive. It can describe entire worlds with multiple robots, lighting, and, importantly, **sensors**.

**xacro** is a macro preprocessor for URDF, because raw URDF is extremely verbose and has no way to express "and now the same wheel again, mirrored".

This distinction is the direct cause of three warnings in your log:

```
[sdformat_urdf]: SDFormat link [imu_link] has a <sensor>, but URDF does not support this
[sdformat_urdf]: SDFormat link [base_scan] has a <sensor>, but URDF does not support this
```

The ASL robot is defined in SDF, because Gazebo needs to simulate its sensors. But `robot_state_publisher` is a ROS node that consumes URDF. So the SDF is converted, and the converter is pointing out that the `<sensor>` tags have no URDF equivalent and are being dropped. That is completely fine: `robot_state_publisher` only needs the kinematics to compute transforms. Gazebo reads the original SDF and simulates the sensors itself.

The other two warnings are of the same family:

```
[kdl_parser]: The root link base_footprint has an inertia specified in the URDF, but
KDL does not support a root link with an inertia.
[kdl_parser]: Converting unknown joint type of joint 'caster_back_joint' into a fixed joint
```

**KDL** (Kinematics and Dynamics Library) is the maths library that turns the description into transforms. It has its own modelling assumptions, one being that the root link carries no inertia, since nothing is attached above it to be affected. The model specifies one anyway, so KDL ignores it. Similarly, the rear caster is a `floating` joint in the model, which KDL cannot represent, so it treats it as fixed. Both are upstream modelling choices interacting with library limitations, neither affects your work, and both appear on every student's screen. Your log even shows the consequence explicitly: `Floating joint. Not adding segment from base_link to caster_back_link.`

The lesson generalises. **A large fraction of ROS warnings are two tools with slightly different data models talking to each other.** Learning which ones are structural and ignorable is a real skill, and the fastest route to it is to read them once carefully, as you are doing now, and then stop reading them.

## 7. Packages, workspaces, and the build system

### 7.1 Packages

A **package** is the unit of ROS software: a directory containing code, plus a `package.xml` declaring its name, version, licence, and dependencies. Your workspace built eighteen of them, including the five from Stanford:

- `asl_tb3_sim` — the simulation, worlds, and launch files
- `asl_tb3_driver` — talking to a real TurtleBot
- `asl_tb3_lib` — shared library code you will write against
- `asl_tb3_msgs` — custom message types for the course
- `asl_tb3_examples` — example nodes

That `asl_tb3_msgs` exists as its own package is itself a lesson: **message definitions are compiled**, and both sides need the generated code, so shared types live in their own package that other packages depend on.

### 7.2 rosdep

`package.xml` lists dependencies by ROS name. **rosdep** is the tool that maps those names to actual system packages for your OS, then installs them. When it ran on your machine it resolved and installed twenty, including `ros-humble-slam-toolbox`, `ros-humble-xacro` and several Gazebo development libraries.

This is worth understanding because of a mistake made during your install, which is instructive. `rosdep` was first run inside a container started with `--rm`, meaning the container is deleted on exit. It reported complete success, and the compiled workspace survived because it was written to a directory shared with WSL. But every package `rosdep` had apt-installed lived *inside* the container filesystem and was destroyed. The failure surfaced only later, as:

```
[ERROR] [launch]: "package 'slam_toolbox' not found, searching: [...]"
```

The fix was to move those twenty packages into the Dockerfile, so they are baked into the image itself rather than installed into a running container. The general principle applies far beyond this one bug: **in container work, always know whether a change you just made lives in the image or in the container.** Image changes persist and are reproducible. Container changes evaporate on rebuild. This is also why your setup is now more durable than the course doc's: following the doc literally, a student who ever rebuilds their container loses `slam_toolbox` and gets that error with no idea why.

### 7.3 colcon, and the overlay model

**colcon** is the build tool. `colcon build` finds every package under `src/`, works out the dependency order, and builds them all. It produces three directories:

- `build/` — intermediate object files, disposable
- `install/` — the finished, usable output
- `log/` — build logs

The flag your course specifies, `--symlink-install`, makes `install/` contain symbolic links back to your source files rather than copies. For Python nodes this means **you can edit the file and re-run immediately without rebuilding**, which massively tightens the loop when you are working on an assignment. You still must rebuild after changing C++ code, adding a new file, or editing `package.xml`.

Now the part that explains the two lines in your `.bashrc`. ROS uses a layered environment model:

- The **underlay** is your system ROS: `/opt/ros/humble`. It holds the 240-odd standard packages.
- An **overlay** is a workspace built on top: your `~/tb_ws`. It holds your 18.

Sourcing a setup script sets environment variables so the shell can find packages. Overlays stack, and later ones take precedence, so a package in your workspace shadows one of the same name in the underlay. That is a feature: it is how you patch an upstream package by cloning it into your own workspace.

```bash
source /opt/ros/humble/setup.bash
source $HOME/tb_ws/install/local_setup.bash
```

The distinction between the two script names catches people out. `setup.bash` sources a workspace *and everything beneath it*. `local_setup.bash` sources *only that workspace*. The pair above is the correct idiom: underlay in full, then your workspace on top.

**Every new terminal needs this.** Which is exactly why those lines are in your `.bashrc` rather than something you type. If you ever see `Package 'asl_tb3_sim' not found` in a shell where it worked yesterday, the near-certain cause is an unsourced environment.

## 8. Launch files: one command, many processes

Starting seven nodes by hand in seven terminals, in the right order, each with the right parameters, is intolerable. A **launch file** describes a set of processes to start, with their parameters, remappings and namespaces, and starts them together. In ROS 2 launch files are Python programs, which means they can contain logic: conditionals, loops, arguments.

`ros2 launch asl_tb3_sim root.launch.py` means "find the package `asl_tb3_sim`, look in its share directory for `root.launch.py`, execute it". Here is what yours started, and why each exists:

| Process | Job |
|---|---|
| `gz sim` | The physics simulator and its 3D window |
| `create` | Spawns the TurtleBot model into the running simulation, then exits |
| `robot_state_publisher` | Reads the robot description, publishes `/tf` and `/robot_description` |

It is a **one-shot** node. Its entire job is to ask the running simulator to insert the robot, confirm it worked, and exit. A process disappearing thirty seconds into your session is not always a crash; sometimes it has simply finished. `finished cleanly` is the phrase to look for.

## 9. Simulation: Gazebo, and why a bridge exists

**Gazebo** is a physics simulator: rigid-body dynamics, collisions, and simulated sensors that produce plausible data from the simulated world. Your laser scans come from ray-casting against simulated geometry.

Gazebo has a confusing history, and you should know the shape of it because the names appear everywhere in your logs and file paths. The original Gazebo (now "Gazebo Classic") was superseded by a rewrite called **Ignition**, which was then renamed **back** to Gazebo in 2022 for trademark reasons. So the modern product has releases named alphabetically — Fortress, Garden, Harmonic — while its libraries and command-line tool are called `gz`, and a great deal of code still says `ign`.

This is why your workspace contains both `ros_gz_bridge` and `ros_ign_bridge`: the same code under both names, kept for compatibility. It is also why your launch line reads:

```
ruby /usr/bin/gz sim -r .../turtlebot3_world.sdf --force-version 7
```

Garden is `gz-sim` version 7, hence `--force-version 7`. And `GZ_VERSION=garden` had to be set before you built, because `ros_gz` compiles against whichever Gazebo version that variable names. Build it against the wrong one and you get link errors that are miserable to diagnose. That is why the variable is exported in your `.bashrc`.

### Why a bridge is needed

Here is the thing that surprises people: **Gazebo is not a ROS program.** It is an independent simulator with its own message types and its own transport layer. It can be used with no ROS at all.

So something must translate. `ros_gz_bridge` (running as `parameter_bridge` in your log) subscribes on one side and republishes on the other, converting message types as it goes. Your log lists every bridged topic, and the format is precise:

```
Creating ROS->GZ Bridge: [/cmd_vel (geometry_msgs/msg/Twist) -> /cmd_vel (gz.msgs.Twist)]
Creating GZ->ROS Bridge: [/lidar (gz.msgs.LaserScan) -> /scan (sensor_msgs/msg/LaserScan)]
Creating GZ->ROS Bridge: [/odom (gz.msgs.Odometry) -> /odom (nav_msgs/msg/Odometry)]
```

Read those carefully and you learn three things. First, direction matters: `/cmd_vel` flows ROS to Gazebo (you commanding the robot), while sensor data flows Gazebo to ROS. Second, **names can differ across the bridge**: Gazebo calls it `/lidar`, ROS calls it `/scan`. Third, types are genuinely converted, `gz.msgs.LaserScan` into `sensor_msgs/msg/LaserScan`, not merely relabelled.

The payoff of this architecture is the one that matters for the course: **your code does not know it is in a simulation.** It subscribes to `/scan` and publishes `/cmd_vel`. When the course moves to a physical TurtleBot, `asl_tb3_driver` replaces Gazebo and the bridge, publishing the same `/scan` from a real laser and consuming the same `/cmd_vel` to drive real motors. Your node is unchanged. That substitutability is the entire reason for the indirection, and it is the strongest practical argument for ROS as a whole.

## 10. SLAM: what slam_toolbox is doing

**SLAM** is Simultaneous Localisation and Mapping, and the name states the chicken-and-egg problem exactly. To build a map you must know where you are. To know where you are you need a map. SLAM solves both at once.

Why you cannot just use wheel odometry: wheels slip, and every small error integrates. Drive a robot in a 10-metre square using only wheel counts and it will believe it has returned to the start while standing a metre away. The error is unbounded, and it only grows.

The laser fixes this, because the world provides a reference. If the wall pattern you see now matches one you saw earlier, you can correct your position estimate.

`slam_toolbox` implements **pose-graph SLAM**, and the mental model is worth having:

1. Every so often it records a **node** in a graph: a robot pose plus the laser scan taken there.
2. It adds **edges** between nodes as constraints: "from pose 5 to pose 6 the robot moved 30 cm forward", derived from odometry and from **scan matching**, which finds the rigid transform that best aligns two scans.
3. When the robot revisits somewhere it has been, it adds a **loop closure** edge, which is the high-value one.
4. It then solves for the set of poses minimising total constraint error. This is a large sparse least-squares problem, and it is what **Ceres** is for. Your log announced exactly this: `Using solver plugin solver_plugins::CeresSolver` and `CeresSolver: Using SCHUR_JACOBI preconditioner`.
5. The map is then rendered by projecting every scan from its corrected pose into an **occupancy grid** — a 2D array where each cell holds the probability it is occupied. That is what `/map` carries and what you see in RViz.

The `async_` prefix in `async_slam_toolbox_node` means optimisation runs on a background thread, so a slow solve does not block scan intake. The alternative synchronous node processes every scan in order and is more deterministic but stalls under load.

One last warning from your log, now readable:

```
slam_toolbox: minimum laser range setting (0.0 m) exceeds the capabilities of
the used Lidar (0.1 m)
```

The configuration says to trust readings from 0 metres outward; the simulated laser cannot see closer than 0.1 metres. `slam_toolbox` is noting the mismatch and clamping to the sensor's real limit. It is an upstream configuration nit in the ASL package, it affects nothing you will do, and every student sees it.

## 11. What your installation actually built

Five stacked layers, each solving one problem created by the one below.

```
+------------------------------------------------------------------+
| Windows 11                                                        |
|  +------------------------------------------------------------+  |
|  | WSL 2  -- a real Linux kernel in a lightweight VM           |  |
|  |  distro "Ubuntu" = Ubuntu 22.04.2 LTS, user anshuarora      |  |
|  |  WSLg provides the X display; /dev/dxg exposes the GPU      |  |
|  |  +------------------------------------------------------+  |  |
|  |  | Docker daemon (auto-starts via /etc/wsl.conf)         |  |  |
|  |  |  +------------------------------------------------+  |  |  |
|  |  |  | Container: ROS 2 Development Container         |  |  |  |
|  |  |  |   FROM ros:humble  (Ubuntu 22.04 + ROS Humble) |  |  |  |
|  |  |  |   + RViz, Gazebo Garden, 20 rosdep packages    |  |  |  |
|  |  |  |   +-----------------------------------------+  |  |  |  |
|  |  |  |   | ~/tb_ws  -- your workspace, 18 packages |  |  |  |  |
|  |  |  |   | (bind-mounted, lives outside container) |  |  |  |  |
|  |  |  |   +-----------------------------------------+  |  |  |  |
|  |  |  +------------------------------------------------+  |  |  |
|  |  +------------------------------------------------------+  |  |
|  +------------------------------------------------------------+  |
+------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

**Why WSL.** ROS Humble needs Ubuntu 22.04 binaries. WSL 2 runs a genuine Linux kernel with near-native filesystem performance on its own ext4 volume. Its companion WSLg forwards Linux GUI windows to the Windows desktop, which is what lets RViz and Gazebo appear as ordinary windows.

**Why Docker on top.** A fair question, since WSL is already Ubuntu 22.04 and ROS could be installed directly into it. Three reasons the course chose containers. Reproducibility: every student gets a byte-identical environment, so a teaching assistant debugging your problem is looking at the same system. Isolation: ROS installs many system packages, and containing them keeps your personal WSL clean. And disposability: a broken environment is fixed by rebuilding the image rather than by unpicking apt state.

**Why a devcontainer specifically.** A container alone would leave you editing files in one place and running them in another. VS Code's Dev Containers extension runs its own server *inside* the container, so your editor, terminal, debugger and extensions all execute in the ROS environment. The badge reading `Dev Container: ROS 2 Development Container` is the confirmation.

**Why the workspace is bind-mounted.** The container's `/home` is your WSL home directory, mounted through. The consequence is that your compiled workspace physically lives on WSL's disk, not inside the container. Rebuilding the container therefore cannot destroy your work. This is also the source of the layout quirk worth remembering: inside the container the workspace is `~/tb_ws`; from a plain WSL terminal the same files are at `~/anshuarora/tb_ws`, because the container's home is one level below the mount point.

**What the GPU work added.** WSL exposes the GPU as `/dev/dxg`, not the `/dev/dri` a normal Linux box uses, which is why the course doc's commented-out `/dev/dri` line would never have worked. Passing `/dev/dxg` plus the driver libraries in `/usr/lib/wsl` lets Mesa's Direct3D-12 backend offer hardware OpenGL. One catch: by default it selects the integrated AMD GPU, so `MESA_D3D12_DEFAULT_ADAPTER_NAME=NVIDIA` is needed to reach the RTX 3050 Ti. Verified renderer:

```
OpenGL renderer string: D3D12 (NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 Ti Laptop GPU)
OpenGL version string: 4.2 (Compatibility Profile) Mesa 23.2.1
```

Gazebo needs OpenGL 3.3; 4.2 is comfortable. Keep the expectation calibrated, though: physics is CPU-bound, so hardware rendering mainly buys a smoother viewport. Measured real-time factor moved from about 0.58 to about 0.78, and dropped scans went from one to zero. Real, but modest.

### The state on disk

| Thing | Where | What |
|---|---|---|
| Distro | WSL `Ubuntu` | Ubuntu 22.04.2 LTS |
| Container config | `~/.devcontainer/` | `devcontainer.json` and `Dockerfile` |
| Image | `cs237a-ros2:humble` | 4.81 GB, ROS Humble + Gazebo Garden + deps |
| Workspace | `~/anshuarora/tb_ws` (WSL) = `~/tb_ws` (container) | 18 built packages |
| Shell setup | container `~/.bashrc` | Sources ROS and the workspace, sets `GZ_VERSION` |

### One rule worth stating plainly

Your WSL home contains a Python virtual environment, `ml-gpu`, for your machine-learning work. **Do not activate it inside the ROS container.** Two independent reasons. Its `activate` script hardcodes `/home/anshuarora/ml-gpu`, which does not resolve inside the container because of the mount layout, so you get a venv that reports itself active while pointing at nothing. More importantly, activating any virtual environment inside the ROS container puts a different Python interpreter and `site-packages` ahead of the one ROS was built against, which breaks `ros2` imports in ways that are genuinely painful to diagnose.

Keep them separate: machine-learning work in a plain WSL terminal, CS237A in the container. Auto-activation has now been disabled for this workspace so VS Code will not do it behind your back.

**This is the line that looks like a failure and is not.** On Linux, a negative exit code reported this way means the process was terminated by a signal, and the magnitude is the signal number. Two habits worth forming from this. **Read the exit code, not the severity label.** And **check whether the other processes finished cleanly**: five did, which is not what a genuine crash looks like.

## 14. Glossary

**Action** — Goal, feedback, result, cancel. For long-running tasks.

**colcon** — The build tool. `colcon build` compiles every package under `src/`.

**DDS** — The industrial publish/subscribe standard ROS 2 uses for transport and discovery. Replaces ROS 1's master.

**Gazebo / gz / Ignition** — The physics simulator. Renamed twice; all three names refer to the same lineage. Garden is `gz-sim` version 7.

**KDL** — Kinematics and Dynamics Library. Turns a robot description into transforms.

**Launch file** — A Python program that starts many nodes with their configuration.

**Message** — A typed data structure sent over a topic. `sensor_msgs/msg/LaserScan` is a type.

**Node** — One program doing one job.

**Occupancy grid** — A 2D array where each cell holds the probability that it is occupied. The map format.

**Overlay / underlay** — Layered workspaces. `/opt/ros/humble` is the underlay; `~/tb_ws` overlays it and takes precedence.

**Package** — The unit of ROS software. A directory with a `package.xml`.

**Parameter** — A named configuration value belonging to a node.

**Pose** — Position plus orientation. Six numbers in 3D, three in 2D.

**QoS** — Quality of Service. Per-connection delivery policy. Mismatched profiles silently fail to connect.

**rosdep** — Maps dependencies declared in `package.xml` to system packages and installs them.

**RViz** — The 3D visualiser. Displays what the robot believes, not ground truth, which is exactly what makes it useful for debugging.

**SDF / URDF / xacro** — Gazebo's robot-and-world format, ROS's robot format, and the macro preprocessor for URDF.

**Service** — Request and response, one-to-one, caller waits.

**SLAM** — Simultaneous Localisation and Mapping.

**tf2** — The coordinate-frame system. Maintains a timestamped tree of transforms, queryable at any past instant.

**Topic** — A named, typed, many-to-many message stream.

**Twist** — The message type for velocity: linear and angular components. What `/cmd_vel` carries.

**WSL / WSLg** — Windows Subsystem for Linux, and its GUI forwarding companion.

## 15. What to learn next

**The single most valuable habit** is inspecting a running system from a second terminal. Start the simulator, open another terminal, and use `ros2 topic list`, `ros2 topic echo`, `ros2 topic hz` and `ros2 node info`. Students who treat the running system as observable debug in minutes; students who only read source code debug in hours.

**Next, drive the robot.** Start the simulator and run `teleop_twist_keyboard` in a second terminal. Watch the map build in RViz as you drive. This makes the whole `/cmd_vel` to `/odom` to `/scan` to `/map` loop concrete in about ten minutes, and it is the loop every assignment will sit inside.

**Then write a node that subscribes and publishes.** The archetypal first exercise: subscribe to `/scan`, find the nearest obstacle, publish a `/cmd_vel` that stops before hitting it. That is a complete robot controller, and everything the course adds later is a more sophisticated version of the same shape.

**Read the ASL examples.** `asl_tb3_examples` exists to show you the house style, and `asl_tb3_lib` holds the base classes your assignments will inherit from. Reading them before the first assignment is an hour well spent.

**When something breaks, in this order:** Is the environment sourced in *this* terminal? Is the node actually running (`ros2 node list`)? Is data actually flowing (`ros2 topic hz`)? Is the transform tree connected (`view_frames`)? Do the QoS profiles match? That sequence catches the overwhelming majority of problems, and it is roughly ordered by how often each is the culprit.

One closing note on what today did and did not accomplish. The environment is built, verified, and more durable than the course doc's default, because the dependencies are baked into the image rather than installed into a disposable container. What it does not give you is familiarity. The fastest way to acquire that is the second terminal: run the simulator, poke it from the side, and watch what the numbers do.
