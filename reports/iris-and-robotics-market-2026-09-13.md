# IRIS, the Robotics Market, and Whether There Is a Company Here

*A decision document assembled from three independent research passes: a verified roster of every current member of Stanford's IRIS lab with what each actually works on, a sector-by-sector map of where robotics makes money today and where it could, and an evidence pass on dexterity and deformables. A fourth pass then synthesized the three against one specific question, which is whether the direction selected on 7 September 2026 supports a research career, a company, or only the former. Every figure is labeled by source type. Company self-reports are marked as such. Where a number could not be verified, the text says so rather than substituting a plausible one, because the reader is making a career decision on this.*

## Executive Summary

**The short version.** The direction you chose is correct as research and unproven as a business, and those are two separate decisions that this document deliberately keeps apart. Evaluation and verification is genuinely thin inside IRIS, not crowded: the lab has six or more papers in the area but its members are nearly always middle authors, and the only member who owns any of it leaves for a faculty post in 2027. That is an orphaned thrust with demonstrated lab appetite and no internal claimant, which is the most favorable configuration a newcomer can find. Independently, a market pass that did not know this was your direction graded certification and evaluation infrastructure for learned controllers as the strongest of eight falsifiable hypotheses, and noted without prompting that it is unusually well matched to an estimation and statistics background. So the research bet is well founded and you should stop re-opening it.

**The business bet is weaker than the 7 September session credited, and two arguments do most of the damage.** The first is the commons argument. Every sophisticated actor who has felt this pain built the tool internally and gave it away: Toyota Research Institute open-sourced [lbm_eval](https://github.com/ToyotaResearchInstitute/lbm_eval) with 49 tasks, Berkeley open-sourced [RoboArena](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.18123), and [Bi-DexHands](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08686), [DaXBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13066), [GarmentLab](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.01200) and [SoftGym](https://sites.google.com/view/softgym) all did the same. The field has priced evaluation tooling at zero every single time it has had the chance to price it. The second is the self-liquidation argument, which is subtler and worse. The one deployment-grade result in the entire evidence set is DYNA at 99.4 percent towel folding across 850-plus napkins and 99 percent quality acceptance across 200,000-plus towels. When reliability is real, trial counts arrive free, because the robot runs 200,000 times in production. Statistically valid evaluation is therefore cheap exactly where the business works and expensive exactly where it does not.

**What survives that attack is narrower than "robot evaluation" and it is the part worth building.** Production telemetry tells you about the deployed policy on the deployed distribution. It cannot tell you whether an over-the-air update is a regression, because you cannot run the previous policy counterfactually in production without surrendering throughput. The measurement problem at the moment of model update does not self-liquidate, and it is getting harder rather than easier: required rollouts scale as one over delta squared, so resolving a 5 point difference needs roughly 1,570 rollouts per arm and 2 points needs roughly 9,800, which means the problem grows as policies converge. That, and not general evaluation, is the defensible thesis.

**The corroborating spending record is real, and it is the strongest single fact in this document.** Toyota Research Institute ran [1,800 real-world rollouts and over 47,000 simulation rollouts across 29 tasks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05331) with sequential hypothesis testing and a blind A/B protocol, then published the conclusion that their own confidence intervals were 20 to 30 percentage points wide and that many robotics papers may be measuring statistical noise. That is a well-resourced industrial lab that already bought the robot-hours and received an uninterpretable answer. Physical Intelligence is doing the same thing at a smaller trial count: [RECAP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.14759) claims roughly halved failure rates using 300 trajectories per iteration on simple laundry, and halving a 10 percent failure rate is a 5 point effect needing roughly 1,570 rollouts per arm, so the headline is underpowered by its own budget by a factor of two to five. These are people already spending money badly on the problem, which is the exact standard your own September postmortem established.

**The capability evidence explains why this matters now.** The cleanest signal in the dexterity pass is that [Gemini Robotics 2](https://deepmind.google/blog/gemini-robotics-2-brings-whole-body-intelligence-to-robots/) unscrews a lightbulb 92 percent of the time and screws the same bulb back in 36 percent of the time, with the same model and the same hand. Insertion and compliance are the blocker, not perception. [ALOHA Unleashed](https://aloha-unleashed.github.io/assets/aloha_unleashed.pdf) spent 26,000 real episodes across 35 operators, 10 robots and 8 months to reach 40 percent on messy shoelaces, and its data ablation is a cliff rather than a curve: 8,658 demonstrations gives 70 percent on the hard shirt variant, 4,329 gives 70 percent, and 2,164 gives zero. Nobody has characterized where that knee sits or how to estimate it cheaply, which is a research opening in its own right.

**The two risks you are currently underweighting are institutional, not technical.** Chelsea Finn co-founded Physical Intelligence, and the roster shows Physical Intelligence is the lab's dominant industry channel. An honest measurement programme produces findings adverse to policy vendors, and the two sharpest available findings are that [pi-zero-point-five](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.16054v1) reports task progress rather than success rate, and that RECAP's claim is underpowered by its own budget. An independent evaluator of robot policies is structurally adversarial to the people who ship policies. This is not a disclosure problem, it is a thesis-selection problem, and on this single axis Shuran Song's REALab is a materially cleaner home than IRIS. Second, master's latitude is narrower than a postdoc's description of it implies: of eight current IRIS MS students, roughly half have a visible paper, the modal path is second or third author inside a senior member's agenda, and one first-author paper is a realistic ceiling across six quarters.

**The recommendation, stated plainly.** Take the research, defer the company, and run three cheap falsification tests first. The single highest-value project is not the most sophisticated one; it is the failure-definition project, because its first experiment completes in three to five weeks on existing rollout video with zero robot hours, and because the field is already quietly substituting metrics to conceal partial failure. Run the three interview tests in [Section 4](#4-synthesis-iris-research-market-gaps-and-a-decision) before committing capital or identity to the startup, pre-register the go-or-no-go binary before the first call, and do not let the co-founder source the first ten conversations, because the prior failure is direct evidence that this sourcing channel yields contaminated samples. My full reasoning, including where I think the co-founder arrangement is upside down, is in [Section 5](#5-closing-what-you-now-know-what-to-study-next-and-my-recommendations).

### Postdocs

**Yuejiang Liu** works on the idea that verifying a prediction is fundamentally easier than making one, and builds verifiers and critics that supply dense feedback where reward is scarce. His first-author [World Action Verifier](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01985) decomposes action-conditioned prediction into state plausibility and action reachability, arguing each is separately checkable because action-free data is abundant and action-relevant features are low-dimensional, and it won Best Paper at the ICLR 2026 World Model workshop. He is co-first author on [LLM-as-a-Verifier](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05391) and first author on [Bidirectional Decoding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.17355) at ICLR 2025, co-first with Jubayer Ibn Hamid. His work is simulation-led, using MiniGrid, RoboMimic and ManiSkill with small real-robot checks, and he is mentored by Yilun Du alongside Finn. **His own page states he is an incoming Assistant Professor at the National University of Singapore starting 2027 and is already recruiting students.** He is the single clearest departure in the lab.

**Ji Woong Kim** asks whether an imitation-learned policy can execute a complete surgical procedure autonomously on real hardware, including recovering from its own errors. [SRT-H](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10251) answers this affirmatively for hierarchical language-conditioned imitation and appeared in Science Robotics 2025 with a July cover, and it is the lab's highest-prestige recent placement. He is co-first author with MS student Ke Wang on [Ego-Pi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08107) at CVPR 2026, which fine-tunes a VLA on egocentric human video and controls a humanoid with Tesollo and Inspire five-finger hands over a 58-dimensional bimanual action space. His hardware is the da Vinci and dVRK surgical platform plus humanoids with dexterous hands. Axel Krieger at Johns Hopkins is the senior collaborator rather than a Stanford co-advisor. His page solicits graduate students and PhD visitors, which reads as faculty-track, though no accepted position is stated.

### PhD students

**Perry Dong** is the lab's highest-volume author by a wide margin, with eight first-author papers in roughly eighteen months. His question is how to make value-based reinforcement learning work when the policy is a diffusion or flow model and the critic is transformer-scale. Representative work is [EXPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07986) at ICLR 2026 on stabilising RL with expressive policies, [Q-Learning With World Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17163) on test-time world-model search for action selection, [FASTER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19730) co-first with Alexander Swerdlow on value-guided filtering inside the denoising loop, and [What Matters for Batch Online RL in Robotics](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.08078). He is listed on the ILIAD people page as co-advised by **Dorsa Sadigh and Chelsea Finn**. Work is simulation-heavy on Robomimic and LIBERO with some real single-arm manipulation. His start year is UNVERIFIED; no Stanford profile resolves and even his own site root returns 404.

**Yoonho Lee** has pivoted cleanly from robustness to language-model systems. His question now is how to improve an LLM system without touching its weights, by optimising the text and code artifacts around it. [Meta-Harness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28052) at COLM 2026 automates search over harness source code using execution traces, with Omar Khattab as co-author and undergraduate Roshen Nair as second author. [Feedback Descent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.07919) argues pairwise critiques carry more supervisory bandwidth than binary preferences. His earlier cluster, [Project and Probe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03715) and hypothesis reweighting in [Test-Time Alignment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.08812), was the lab's robustness line. Advised by **Finn alone**, LLM-only with no robot, and **his site states explicitly that he is on the 2026 to 2027 academic and industry job market.**

**Moo Jin Kim** asks how to turn large pretrained generative models into fast, high-success robot policies. He is co-first author on [OpenVLA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09246), a CoRL 2024 Outstanding Paper finalist where a 7B open model beats a 55B closed one, first author on [OpenVLA-OFT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.19645) at RSS 2025 which isolates parallel decoding, action chunking and L1 regression to reach 97.1 percent on LIBERO with 26 times faster inference, and first author on [Cosmos Policy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16163) at ICLR 2026, an NVIDIA collaboration that adapts a video diffusion model by encoding actions and values as latent frames. Hardware spans WidowX, Google Robot, Franka Panda and bimanual ALOHA. **Co-advised by Finn and Percy Liang**, stated on his site, and final-year. Notably his site says he now works on LLM post-training for Marin, Stanford's open foundation model, so he has moved off robotics.

**Jonathan Yang** attacks the question of which kind of data diversity buys which kind of generalisation. [Data Analogies](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06450) makes the sharp claim that viewpoint shifts benefit from broad unstructured diversity while morphology shifts do not, and instead gain most from paired cross-embodiment demonstrations, improving real transfer by 22.5 percent purely through data composition. [Invariance Co-training](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.05230) co-trains on cheap Unreal Engine imagery with perturbation-invariance objectives, and [Bridging Perception and Action](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06196) supervises mid-level object-centric and pose-aware representations through a mixture of experts feeding a diffusion policy. **Co-advised by Finn and Sadigh**, stated on his site, admitted Autumn 2022. The Google DeepMind robotics author list on the 2025 paper suggests an internship, which I mark as inference.

**Tian Gao** works on bridging high-level semantic reasoning and low-level reactive control. He is first author on [SteerVLA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08440), which uses a VLM to emit fine-grained language instructions that steer a low-level driving VLA, the key move being VLM-generated dense language annotations as the supervision that makes the language interface actually steerable, for a gain of 8.04 on long-tail scenarios. He is second author on [CHORUS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.12352), which shows one shared VLA backbone can drive decentralised multi-robot teams from purely local observations plus a robot-identifying prompt, at 90 percent success on three-robot teams. **Co-advised by Finn and Sadigh**, stated on his site, which corrects an easy misreading: Jeannette Bohg co-authors CHORUS but does not advise him. Tsinghua Yao Class background; start year UNVERIFIED.

**Anikait Singh** asks what data and feedback actually make preference fine-tuning work, and how a model adapts to one real user from a handful of examples. [FSPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.19312) reframes reward modelling as meta-learning over synthetic preference distributions and reports a 70 percent win rate with real humans. [Preference Fine-Tuning of LLMs Should Leverage Suboptimal, On-Policy Data](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14367) is the ICML 2024 result the title states, and he is second author on [RLAD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.02263), an ICLR 2026 Spotlight. **Co-advised by Finn and Aviral Kumar**, stated verbatim on his site. LLM-only. Final-year, and his site states he is currently a Research Scientist Intern at Meta Superintelligence Labs.

**Lucy Xiaoyang Shi** asks how a robot turns open-ended language, including mid-task corrections, into long-horizon behaviour, and increasingly how to close the loop against a learned world model. [Hi Robot](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.19417) at ICML 2025 is her hierarchical VLA on single-arm, dual-arm and mobile platforms doing table cleaning and sandwich making, [Yell At Your Robot](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12910) is live language correction on ALOHA-style bimanual hardware, and she is co-first with Yanjiang Guo on [Ctrl-World](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.10125) at ICLR 2026 and co-first on [VLAW](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12063) at ICML 2026, which co-improves policy and world model for a 39.2 point absolute gain. Started September 2024, advised by **Finn alone**, and a researcher at Physical Intelligence since March 2024 as one of its first team members.

**Marcel Torne Villasevil** asks how policies adapt in context with human-centric supervision. He is first author on [MEM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03596) at CoRL 2026, which mixes video-encoded short-horizon memory with text-based long-horizon memory to run fifteen-minute tasks like cleaning a kitchen, on [Freeform Preference Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.32027) where annotators define their own language preference axes such as speed and carefulness for a 38 point gain over binary-preference baselines, and on [Past-Token Prediction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09561) at CoRL 2025 for 3 times performance at 10 times faster training. Also first author on [CASHER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01770), real-to-sim-to-real with super-linear scaling in human effort. Started September 2024, advised by **Finn alone**; Pulkit Agrawal is a prior MIT supervisor and continuing collaborator, not an advisor. He was at Physical Intelligence from June 2025 to March 2026.

**Lars Lien Ankile** asks how to squeeze precision out of imperfect behaviour-cloned policies by treating the BC policy as a frozen black box and learning lightweight per-step residual corrections with off-policy RL from sparse binary rewards. [Residual Off-Policy RL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19301) at ICRA 2026 claims the first successful real-world RL training on a humanoid robot with dexterous hands. [From Imitation to Refinement](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.16677) at ICRA 2025 and [JUICER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.03729) at IROS 2024 are precise visual assembly on Franka-class arms. **Admitted Autumn 2025 per Stanford Profiles, advised by Finn alone**, so he is very early. Interned at Amazon Frontier AI and Robotics. He appears on no 2024 to 2026 entry of the IRIS publications page, which is a lag in that page rather than an absence of work.

**Jubayer Ibn Hamid** has pivoted from robot action chunking to LLM post-training, asking how to train a model to use all three axes of inference compute jointly. [SPIRAL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.23595) trains sequential reasoning, parallel sampling and learned aggregation end to end against the aggregated reward, using set-level RL so parallel traces are collectively rather than individually useful, for up to 11 times better scaling efficiency than GRPO. He co-leads [Poly-EPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.17654) and [Polychromic Objectives](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25424) with Ifdita Hasan Orney, and was co-first on Bidirectional Decoding. **Co-advised by Sadigh and Finn**, stated on his site, with Noah Goodman a recurring third senior author. Admitted Autumn 2025 and a former IRIS undergraduate. LinkedIn lists him as a Student Researcher at Google. LLM-only.

**Ajay Sridhar** asks how to give policies the two things monolithic VLAs lack, memory and embodiment-invariance. [MemER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.20328) at ICLR 2026 retrieves task-relevant keyframes from past experience instead of stuffing the context window, built on Qwen2.5-VL-7B and pi-zero-point-five. [BARX](https://ajaysridhar.com/barx/) at ICRA 2026 is co-first with Jensen Gao on behaviour-aligned cross-embodiment representations with Toyota Research Institute, up 28 percent. He was first author on [NoMaD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07896), an ICRA 2024 Best Conference Paper. **Co-advised by Sadigh and Finn**, stated on his site, NSF Fellow. Real hardware is ViperX 300 S and Franka Research 3. Start year UNVERIFIED. BARX has no arXiv ID I could locate.

**Alexander Swerdlow** came from CMU generative modelling into IRIS reinforcement learning. His own line is unified multimodal generation via discrete diffusion in [UniDisc](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20853) and [UniVLG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.10745), co-first with Ayush Jain. Physical Intelligence intern. No robot hardware of his own.

### Thrusts, and where they are crowded versus thin

**Reinforcement learning for expressive policies is the most crowded thrust and the most single-person dominated.** Perry Dong is first author on eight of roughly eleven papers here, with Alexander Swerdlow and MS student Armaan Abraham contributing and Sadigh co-authoring nearly all. The open question is stable, sample-efficient value learning when the policy is a diffusion model and the critic is transformer-scale. A newcomer here competes directly with the most productive author in the lab on his own topic, at a publication cadence that makes incremental work hard to land.

**Generalist policies and VLAs is crowded by headcount and gated by resources.** Moo Jin Kim, Tian Gao, Jonathan Yang, Marcel Torne, Lucy Shi, Ji Woong Kim, Ke Wang and Bo Ai all touch it across OpenVLA, OpenVLA-OFT, Cosmos Policy, CHORUS, SteerVLA, Hi Robot, MEM and Ego-Pi, which is nine or more papers. The open question is how one policy generalises across embodiments, teams and long tails. The barrier is not just headcount: several of these are effectively Physical Intelligence or NVIDIA papers with large industrial author lists and privileged data access, which an entering MS student cannot match.

**LLM post-training and inference-time compute is crowded today but scheduled to empty.** Yoonho Lee, Anikait Singh and Jubayer Ibn Hamid account for six or more 2025 and 2026 papers, with Sohyeon Kim and Aneesh Muppidi adjacent. The open question is how to train a model to allocate search, parallelism and aggregation rather than be accurate in one pass. The important structural fact is that Lee and Singh are both final-year, so this is the one crowded thrust that will thin out on a one-to-two-year horizon.

**Memory and long-horizon control is the most attractive thin thrust.** Only Marcel Torne, Ajay Sridhar and undergraduate Aadi Shah occupy it, across MEM, Past-Token Prediction and MemER, and the three papers genuinely disagree about the answer: architectural context, training objective, or retrieval. The open question is what a policy should retain from ten minutes ago and how it should be indexed. Torne and Sridhar are both mid-PhD rather than finishing, so the thrust is growing rather than vacating, and there is space beside them.

**World models is thin in a way the publications page disguises.** Both flagship papers, Ctrl-World and VLAW, are co-first-authored by Yanjiang Guo, who is on the **alumni** list. Among current members the owner is Yuejiang Liu, who leaves for a faculty position at NUS in 2027, with Lucy Shi and rotating student Bo Ai adjacent. The open question is whether a learned world model can be trusted enough to plan or to score actions, and World Action Verifier's self-verification answer is one paper old. This is the clearest case of an important thrust with a departing owner.

**Surgical and medical robotics is thin and entirely transient.** Ji Woong Kim is a postdoc and Gary Sarwin is a visiting PhD student from ETH, so the thrust with the lab's highest-prestige venue placement has no permanent occupant and no succession plan. The open question is how far language-conditioned imitation can go on real surgical hardware before safety verification becomes the binding constraint.

**Evaluation, verification and benchmarking is thin inside IRIS despite a healthy paper count.** RoboArena, PolaRiS, RoboMME, SutureBot, LLM-as-a-Verifier and DIRECT all exist, but IRIS members are usually middle authors: RoboArena is led from Berkeley, the verification papers by Jacky Kwok with Marco Pavone and Azalia Mirhoseini, RoboMME from Michigan. Only Yuejiang Liu owns any of it, and he is leaving. The open question of how to compare generalist policies without a fixed environment is both important and unclaimed.

**Dexterous, contact-rich and humanoid manipulation is thin and newly opened.** Lars Ankile is essentially the whole thrust, with Ji Woong Kim's Ego-Pi and Bo Ai's dexterous world models adjacent. Ankile was admitted Autumn 2025, so a newcomer would be arriving alongside rather than behind him. **Human preference learning is similarly thin** and unusually accessible, since Freeform Preference Learning is Marcel Torne plus two MS students.

Two hypothesised thrusts are effectively vacant. **Robustness, distribution shift and test-time adaptation** was a major 2024 line, all Yoonho Lee, and it emptied when he pivoted to LLM systems; Jonathan Yang's Invariance Co-training is the only successor. **Theory and representation learning** is gone entirely, since Kyle Hsu and Will Dorrell are both alumni.

### Departure, internship and status signals

The lab site states none of this; all of it comes from personal pages and Stanford Profiles. The confirmed departure is **Yuejiang Liu**, incoming Assistant Professor at NUS in 2027 and already recruiting. **Yoonho Lee** states he is on the 2026 to 2027 job market. **Moo Jin Kim** and **Anikait Singh** both describe themselves as final-year, and Singh is currently interning at Meta Superintelligence Labs while Kim has moved onto Stanford's Marin project. **Ji Woong Kim** is soliciting students and visitors, which reads as faculty-track but is unconfirmed. ## 2. Robotics Industry Map, Capability Gaps, and Hypothesis Tests

### How to read this document

Three kinds of number appear below, and they are labelled every time. **Filing** means an audited annual report, 10-K, or earnings release. **Association census** means a trade body that collects supplier-reported unit data, principally the International Federation of Robotics, whose statistics are assembled by VDMA Services from data reported by robot suppliers and national associations including A3, CRIA, JARA and KAR ([World Robotics 2025 executive summary](https://ifr.org/img/worldrobotics/Executive_Summary_WR_2025_Industrial_Robots.pdf)). **Estimate** means a market research firm, and those are named because they disagree by factors of two or more. Where a figure was not verified in this pass, the text says so instead of supplying a plausible substitute. That happens more often than a consulting deck would admit, and the honest gaps are listed at the end.

The organising claim of this document is that robotics has never been limited by manipulation cleverness. It has been limited by the cost of *specifying* a task precisely enough that a machine can repeat it a million times without a human in the loop. Every sector in Part 1 solved that specification problem by changing the environment rather than by making the robot smarter. Every sector in Part 2 has resisted because the environment cannot be changed cheaply enough, or because the task's variance is irreducible.

It succeeded secondarily where the human alternative was priced absurdly, as in mining and cleanrooms. It has never succeeded by making a robot cope with an unmodified human environment. The IFR itself identifies the real bottleneck, and it is not intelligence: "the wide range of engineering capabilities needed also in peripherals, such as vision or process design, often prevent the adoption of robots, especially in SMEs. A developed ecosystem of system integrators that provide these capabilities is key, and quite often the bottleneck" ([IFR World Robotics 2025](https://ifr.org/img/worldrobotics/Executive_Summary_WR_2025_Industrial_Robots.pdf)).

### Part 3: The specific innovation each gap requires

**Gap 1: long-horizon reliability and error recovery.** This is arithmetic before it is research. A task of 50 sequential steps executed at 99 percent per-step reliability completes 0.99 to the fiftieth power of the time, which is about 60.5 percent. To reach 99 percent end-to-end over 50 steps requires per-step reliability of about 99.98 percent. Published manipulation policies do not report per-step figures anywhere near that, and the field's benchmark suites are themselves contested. What is actually needed is not higher nominal success but *detection of failure and recovery from it*, so that a 95 percent step can be retried rather than propagated. This single capability gates unattended operation in warehouse picking, kitchens, sterile processing and construction, because all four lose their economics the moment a human must supervise.

**Gap 2: dexterous manipulation of deformables.** A rigid object has a six-dimensional pose. A bedsheet, a chicken thigh or a wire harness has effectively infinite configuration dimensions and no canonical frame, so the perception problem is not pose estimation but shape estimation under self-occlusion. The engineering requirement is closed-loop control on an estimated deformation field at visual rates, which is precisely a state-estimation problem rather than a policy-learning problem. Unlocks: housekeeping, apparel, meat processing, laundry, harvest. Verified success-rate numbers for cloth and food manipulation from the major labs were **not obtainable in this pass**, and the publicly circulated figures are company demonstrations rather than statistically sampled trials.

**Gap 3: sub-millimetre force-controlled insertion.** Connector mating and precision assembly require positional accuracy tighter than the repeatability of a low-cost arm, so the accuracy must come from force sensing rather than from position. The specific missing capability is cheap, drift-free force and torque sensing at the wrist or joint with resolution fine enough to detect a jam before it becomes damage, combined with a 1 kHz admittance control loop. I **could not verify** specific industry tolerance and force-resolution requirements from a citable source in this pass, and I am not going to invent them, because for this reader they would be load-bearing. Unlocks: electronics final assembly, aerospace fastening, electronics disassembly.

**Gap 4: perception under occlusion and specularity.** Structured-light and time-of-flight depth sensors fail on transparent and mirror-like surfaces because they assume diffuse reflection. Retail packaging, sterile instruments and recycling streams are dominated by exactly those materials. The missing capability is depth estimation that does not assume Lambertian reflectance, which is a physics-of-imaging problem, and it is one where a signal-processing background is a genuine edge. Unlocks: arbitrary-SKU picking, sterile processing, recycling.

**Gap 5: tactile sensing at scale.** Optical tactile sensors exist in research quantities. What does not exist is a tactile skin with industrially credible cycle life, unit cost, and drift characteristics. Specific durability and cost figures were **not verified in this pass**. Without tactile feedback, every contact-rich task is executed open-loop on vision, which is why food and cloth tasks stall.

**Gap 6: safe human-adjacent operation with formal guarantees.** The standards landscape moved in 2025 and matters commercially. ISO 10218 parts 1 and 2 were revised in 2025 after nearly eight years of work, absorbing most of the collaborative-operation content formerly in ISO/TS 15066, introducing Class 1 and Class 2 robot categories, adding cybersecurity requirements, and widening the definition of an industrial robot to include machines "fixed to a mobile platform," which explicitly admits humanoids ([ISO 10218-2:2025](https://www.iso.org/standard/73934.html); [A3 FAQ](https://www.automate.org/robotics/blogs/updated-iso-10218-faq); [The Robot Report](https://www.therobotreport.com/iso-10218-industrial-robot-safety-standard-receives-major-overhaul/)). ISO 13482, covering service and personal care robots, is only now being replaced ([ISO/FDIS 13482](https://www.iso.org/standard/83498.html)). Critically, none of these standards tell you how to certify a *learned* controller whose behaviour is not enumerable. That is an open problem and a business in itself. Unlocks: elder care, hospitality, healthcare, any robot sharing space with the public.

**Gap 7: cheap, statistically valid evaluation.** Robot policy comparison currently rests on trial counts too small to distinguish the policies being compared, and on simulators whose ranking does not transfer. A 10-percentage-point difference in success rate needs on the order of hundreds of physical trials per condition to resolve at conventional confidence, which at realistic cycle times is days of robot occupancy per data point. Without cheap valid evaluation, a company cannot tell whether last month's model is better, which makes engineering management impossible. I **could not verify** specific published trial-count recommendations or sim-to-real correlation coefficients in this pass.

**Gap 8: data.** The largest open cross-robot corpus, Open X-Embodiment, spans 22 robot embodiments from 21 institutions covering 527 skills and 160,266 tasks ([Open X-Embodiment, arXiv 2310.08864](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08864)). That is five to eight orders of magnitude short of the token counts behind language models, and the data is teleoperation-collected, meaning its marginal cost is human hours rather than crawling. There is no credible published scaling law for manipulation that would let a founder forecast how much data buys how much reliability.

**Gap 9: hardware cost, reliability and on-robot compute.** Force control wants roughly 1 kHz loops, which forbids cloud inference, while current vision-language-action policies run at tens of hertz. Any architecture must therefore be hierarchical, with a slow semantic layer and a fast local loop. Specific MTBF requirements, actuator cost curves and humanoid bills of material were **not verified in this pass**; the analyst estimates circulating for humanoid costs differ by more than a factor of three and should not be quoted as fact.

**Gap 10: integration and teaching cost.** This is the gap the industry census itself identifies, quoted at the end of Part 1: integrator capacity, not robot capability, is the adoption bottleneck for small and medium manufacturers ([IFR](https://ifr.org/img/worldrobotics/Executive_Summary_WR_2025_Industrial_Robots.pdf)). Note also that there are 4,663,698 robots installed against a universe of manufacturers where, in the IFR's words, "the share of companies that use robots is still rather small." If a non-expert operator could specify and validate a task in a day, the addressable market would widen by an order of magnitude without a single new manipulation capability.

### Part 4: Hypothesis tests

**H1. Closing arbitrary-SKU piece picking creates real value.** *For:* the warehouse buyer is sophisticated, already automates, and pays for throughput; Symbotic's 22.5 billion dollar backlog proves willingness to commit capital at scale ([Symbotic 10-K](https://www.stocktitan.net/sec-filings/SYM/10-k-symbotic-inc-files-annual-report-b238fde66e2e.html)), and Amazon's own roadmap moved from moving totes to grasping items ([Amazon](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/operations/new-robots-amazon-fulfillment-agentic-ai)). *Against:* the reference wage is 22.44 dollars per hour ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/transportation-and-material-moving/material-moving-machine-operators.htm)) and the incumbent solution deliberately avoided the problem by standardising the tote, which means the buyer has a cheap non-robotic substitute. Every dollar of value requires beating a human on a task humans do well. Verified cost-per-pick benchmarks do not exist publicly, which is itself a warning about how thin the published economics are. *Falsifier:* a third-party audited deployment sustaining above 99.5 percent pick success on unconstrained SKUs at under human cost per pick, replicated at a second customer. Absent that within roughly 24 months, the hypothesis weakens. *Verdict: CONTESTED.*

**H2. Collapsing integration and teaching cost creates real value.** *For:* the IFR names integrator capacity as the bottleneck and notes that the share of firms using robots remains small ([IFR](https://ifr.org/img/worldrobotics/Executive_Summary_WR_2025_Industrial_Robots.pdf)); 4.66 million installed robots imply an enormous installed base of programming work; FANUC's ROBOT division grew 14.9 percent in the year to March 2026 in a flat unit market, which is consistent with value migrating to software and service ([FANUC](https://www.fanuc.co.jp/en/ir/)). *Against:* integrators are the channel, so disintermediating them is a distribution fight; Rethink Robotics attacked exactly this thesis with Baxter and Sawyer, shut in October 2018 with assets going to the HAHN Group, was relaunched in 2024 under new ownership, and failed again in 2025 ([Rethink Robotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rethink_Robotics)). Ease of use did not overcome inadequate accuracy and repeatability. *Falsifier:* if non-expert-programmable cells still require an integrator for validation and safety sign-off, the cost does not actually collapse. *Verdict: STRONG*, with the caveat that Rethink's grave sits directly on it and the lesson is that usability without precision does not sell.

**H3. General-purpose humanoid labour creates real value this cycle.** *For:* capital is overwhelming, with robotics startups raising 18.8 billion dollars in 2026 to date against 15 billion for all of 2025, and humanoids taking 8.6 billion of that, roughly 46 percent ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/robotics/startup-venture-funding-surges-2026-data/)); Figure AI reached a 39 billion dollar post-money valuation in September 2025 ([Figure AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figure_AI)); Agility has a factory in Salem, Oregon and a robot-as-a-service contract with GXO ([Agility Robotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agility_Robotics)). *Against:* no humanoid company has disclosed meaningful revenue. Figure's Wikipedia record shows no revenue figures at a 39 billion dollar valuation, and a Fortune piece in April 2025 questioned how its BMW relationship had been characterised ([Figure AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figure_AI)). Tesla missed its 2023 readiness target and its 2025 limited-production target, was criticised for undisclosed teleoperation at the October 2024 We Robot event, and lost Optimus programme lead Milan Kovac in June 2025 ([Optimus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimus_(robot))). 1X sells NEO with an explicit human-teleoperator "Expert Mode" ([1X](https://www.1x.tech/discover/neo-home-robot); [The Robot Report](https://www.therobotreport.com/1x-announces-pre-order-launch-neo-humanoid-robot/)), which concedes the autonomy is not there. Meanwhile the data foundation is 527 skills and 160,266 tasks across 22 embodiments ([Open X-Embodiment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08864)). *Falsifier:* one audited annual report showing nine figures of humanoid product revenue with gross margin, from a customer who rebought. *Verdict: WEAK* as an investment thesis for a founder, though the component supply chain feeding it is a different and better question.

**H4. Restaurant and commercial kitchen automation creates real value.** *For:* fast food cook wages of 14.85 dollars and 640,500 jobs ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/food-preparation-and-serving/cooks.htm)) plus high turnover make the labour pain real. *Against:* this is the most thoroughly tested negative in robotics. Zume took 375 million dollars from SoftBank in November 2018 at a 2.25 billion dollar valuation to cook pizza in moving vans with robotic cutters; baking in a moving vehicle "proved to be impractical," customers complained about quality, the concept was shelved, and the company shut in June 2023 ([Zume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zume_Pizza)). The failure was not the robot. It was that the robot's output was worse and the fixed cost sat inside a single low-volume store. Fast-food cook employment is projected to grow just 1 percent to 2035 ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/food-preparation-and-serving/cooks.htm)), so there is no tightening-shortage tailwind either. *Falsifier:* a multi-unit operator reporting store-level margin improvement attributable to robotics across 100 or more locations, with rebuy. *Verdict: WEAK.*

**H5. Deformable manipulation for food processing and meat cutting creates real value.** *For:* the buyers are a handful of enormous processors with capital budgets, the work has among the worst injury profiles in manufacturing, and yield optimisation per carcass is a value stream no human can match. *Against:* I **could not verify** the current wage or injury figures for meat cutters in this pass because the BLS occupation page was inaccessible, which weakens my own case. The environment is washdown-rated, cold, and sensor-hostile, and a one percent yield loss can exceed the labour saving, so the machine must be better than a skilled human rather than merely cheaper. No company in this space has disclosed revenue at scale that I verified. *Falsifier:* a processor publicly attributing yield or injury improvement to robotic primal cutting at line rate. *Verdict: CONTESTED*, and this is the most underattacked gap in the document relative to its value, which for a founder is the interesting property.

**H6. Certification and evaluation infrastructure for learned controllers creates real value.** *For:* ISO 10218 was revised in 2025 to admit mobile-platform robots and absorb collaborative-operation requirements ([ISO 10218-2:2025](https://www.iso.org/standard/73934.html); [A3](https://www.automate.org/robotics/blogs/updated-iso-10218-faq)), ISO 13482 is being replaced ([ISO/FDIS 13482](https://www.iso.org/standard/83498.html)), and neither addresses how to certify a policy whose behaviour cannot be enumerated. Every company in H3 and H7 needs this and none can build it alone. Capital is flooding in at 18.8 billion dollars a year ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/robotics/startup-venture-funding-surges-2026-data/)), so the buyers are funded. *Against:* selling tools to a pre-revenue industry means your revenue is a derivative of their funding cycle, which is the classic picks-and-shovels risk in a bubble. Notified bodies and incumbents like TUV and UL may absorb the function. *Falsifier:* a published, regulator-accepted conformity route for learned controllers emerging from the standards bodies themselves, which would eliminate the private opportunity. *Verdict: STRONG*, and it is unusually well matched to an estimation and statistics background, because the deliverable is a statistically defensible reliability claim rather than a mechanism.

**H7. Physical elder care robotics creates real value.** *For:* 4,677,100 jobs, 18 percent projected growth to 2035, and approximately 760,500 annual openings at a 17.21 dollar median wage ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/home-health-aides-and-personal-care-aides.htm)). No sector in this document has a larger structural shortfall. *Against:* the binding constraint is liability and certification, not capability, and ISO 13482 is still in revision ([ISO/FDIS 13482](https://www.iso.org/standard/83498.html)). Payment comes through Medicaid and insurance, which do not reimburse robots. The consumer-facing precedent is dismal: iRobot, the only Western consumer robotics company with real volume, filed Chapter 11 on 14 December 2025 and was sold to Shenzhen Picea Robotics ([iRobot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IRobot)). Cheaper non-robotic substitutes, namely immigration policy and fall-detection sensors, address much of the same pain. *Falsifier:* a reimbursement code for a physical-assistance robot, which would change everything, or its continued absence, which sustains the negative. *Verdict: WEAK* on a founder's ten-year horizon despite being the largest labour gap in the table, because willingness to pay is intermediated by payers who do not pay.

**H8. Geofenced full autonomy creates real value, and the geofence is the product.** *For:* Waymo reached approximately 500,000 paid rides per week and 200 million driverless miles by June 2026, with a 126 billion dollar valuation on 27 billion dollars raised ([Waymo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waymo)). Surface mining, ports and agriculture in Part 1 all confirm the same shape. *Against:* Cruise attacked the same market with more than 10 billion dollars of total funding, had its California permits suspended on 24 October 2023 after a pedestrian was dragged roughly twenty feet, and GM stopped funding the robotaxi programme in December 2024 ([Cruise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruise_(autonomous_vehicle))). Serve Robotics shows the thin-margin version of the same geometry: 2.65 million dollars of 2025 revenue against a roughly 101 million dollar net loss ([Serve Robotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serve_Robotics)). The lesson is that the capital requirement to buy reliability through redundancy is 25 billion dollars, not 25 million, and that below a certain trip value the unit economics never close. *Falsifier:* a second robotaxi operator reaching Waymo-scale paid volume on materially less capital, or Waymo failing to reach positive contribution margin per ride. *Verdict: STRONG* as a validated pattern, but only for founders who can either constrain the environment cheaply, as in mining and agriculture, or command extraordinary capital.

## 3. Dexterity and Deformables: The Evidence Base

Source-type tag on every figure. Company self-reports are labeled. Figures read off bar charts rather than tables are marked with a tilde and carry roughly plus or minus 5 percent reading error.

### The single most important finding for the evaluation thesis

Toyota Research Institute, in [A Careful Examination of Large Behavior Models for Multitask Dexterous Manipulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05331), ran 1,800 real-world rollouts and over 47,000 simulation rollouts across 29 tasks, using 50 rollouts per real task, sequential hypothesis testing, and a blind A/B real-world protocol. Their own stated conclusion is that at 50 rollouts the "confidence interval width is generally 20% to 30% absolute success rate," and that many robotics papers may be measuring "statistical noise due to insufficient statistical power." Their open benchmark is [lbm_eval](https://github.com/ToyotaResearchInstitute/lbm_eval), 49 simulation tasks. Two secondary findings matter as much: there was no consistent outperformance of single-task training without fine-tuning, and data normalization choices dominated architectural changes.

This is an insider admission from a well-resourced industrial lab that the field's standard evaluation protocol cannot support its own claims. It independently validates the arithmetic already on record from the September 3 literature review, and it invalidates cross-paper comparison of nearly every 10-trial and 20-trial success rate cited below, including Physical Intelligence's 10-episode evaluations and ALOHA Unleashed's 20-trial evaluations.

### The cleanest capability signal: removal versus insertion

Gemini Robotics 2 (July 30, 2026, [company self-report](https://deepmind.google/blog/gemini-robotics-2-brings-whole-body-intelligence-to-robots/)), multi-finger dexterity on Apollo with SharpaWave hands: unscrew a lightbulb 92%, screw the same bulb back in 36%. Same model, same hand, same object. Removal is a rigid grasp; insertion is compliant, force-controlled and occluded. Adjacent deformable and granular tasks: tie a trash bag 44%, ziplock 40%, dustpan 32%. Whole-body manipulation with Inspire hands: pick from shelf 76.3%, from table 68.4%, from floor 45.7%. Gripper dexterity on Franka Duo: precise insertion 89.6%, tool kitting 78.9%, general pick and place 74.2%. DeepMind's own post concedes that "multi-finger dexterous manipulation remains challenging."

Gemini Robotics 1.5 (September 2025, [tech report](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/gemini-robotics/Gemini-Robotics-1-5-Tech-Report.pdf)) reports the highest aggregate score across 15 embodied-reasoning benchmarks but publishes no per-task manipulation success rates; shoelace tying and shirt hanging appear as demonstrations only. The same lab's numbered result a year earlier was 40% on messy shoelaces.

### Data scaling: the ALOHA Unleashed ablation

[ALOHA Unleashed](https://aloha-unleashed.github.io/assets/aloha_unleashed.pdf) (CoRL 2024, peer reviewed) collected over 26,000 real episodes for 5 tasks using 35 operators on 10 robots across 2 buildings over 8 months, evaluating 20 trials per task with no partial credit.

| Task | Success | Demos |
|---|---|---|
| Shirt hanging, easy (flat, centered) | 75% | 8,658 |
| Shirt hanging, messy (rotated/crumpled) | 70% | same model |
| Shoelace tying, easy | 70% | 5,133 |
| Shoelace tying, messy (plus/minus 45 degrees) | 40% | same model |
| Robot finger replacement (mm tolerance) | 75% | 5,247 |
| Gear insertion, 1 of 3 | 95% | 4,005 |

The data-scaling ablation is the money finding. ShirtEasy and ShirtMessy at 100% of data (8,658 demos) score 75% and 70%; at 50% (4,329 demos) they score 75% and 70%, which is flat; at 25% (2,164 demos) they score 30% and 0%. Roughly 8,600 human demonstrations buys 70 to 75 percent on a single garment task, and 2,000 buys essentially nothing on the hard variant. Separately, filtering data by episode duration in the low-data regime moved performance from 30% to 55%, so curation beat volume. A baseline Diffusion Policy variant scored 25% on ShirtMessy against 70% for the paper's model.

### Physical Intelligence, in their own documents

[Pi-zero](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.24164v1): over 10,000 hours of robot data, 903M timesteps, 7 platforms, 68 tasks, 3.3B parameters, 50-step action chunks, 73 ms onboard inference. Zero-shot at 10 episodes per task: shirt folding ~0.95, easy bussing ~0.90, grocery bagging ~0.80, toast retrieval ~0.85, hard bussing ~0.65. Laundry folding scored ~0.5 with episodes capped at roughly 5 minutes and 15,000 steps. Per-task fine-tuning ranged from 5 hours to over 100 hours of data.

[Pi-zero-point-five](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.16054v1): roughly 400 hours of mobile manipulation across about 100 home environments, with only 2.4% of first-phase training examples from the target platform. Results in unseen homes are reported as task progress, meaning approximate percent of steps completed, not binary success: items in drawer ~60 to 70%, laundry basket ~50 to 65%, dishes in sink ~55 to 75%, at 10 trials per policy per task. Environment scaling saturates around 100 training environments. The metric substitution from success rate to progress rubric is a materially weaker claim and is easy to miss.

The [pi-zero-point-six model card](https://website.pi-asset.com/pi06star/PI06_model_card.pdf) (November 17, 2025, company self-report) contains the most revealing admission in the set: for laundry folding and box assembly, "previously these two tasks require fine-tuning with high-quality data to achieve non-zero success rates." Pi-zero and pi-zero-point-five scored zero out of the box on both. Pi-zero-point-six "fully assembles the box 20% of the time." Backbone is Gemma3-4B with an 860M action expert, 63 ms per action chunk on one H100. The model card defines throughput as successes per hour.

[RECAP / pi-star-zero-point-six](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.14759) claims more than doubled throughput and roughly halved failure rates on the hardest tasks, with final success in the 90-percent range for espresso, box assembly and simple laundry, though t-shirts and shorts were already near 90% after supervised fine-tuning so the gain there is speed rather than success. Targeted failure removal reached 97% on a strict collar-facing-up requirement after 2 iterations of 600 trajectories. The RL loop cost 300 trajectories on 4 robots per iteration for simple laundry, and 600 autonomous plus 360 intervention trials per iteration for box assembly. Humans still perform reward labeling and interventions, which the paper lists as a limitation. Continuous operation demos: espresso for 13 hours, laundry folding for over 2 hours.

### The sim-to-real gap has not closed in eight years

OpenAI [Dactyl](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.00177): median 50 consecutive block rotations in simulation versus median 13 on physical hardware (mean 18.8 plus or minus 17.1). Vision-based policy: sim median 33, real median 11.5. On an octagonal prism the real median fell to 5, with individual trials ranging from 1 to 50. The cost of that robustness was roughly 100 simulated years of experience with full domain randomization, versus 3 years without, requiring 8 V100 GPUs and 6,144 CPU cores. Withholding observation-noise randomization alone dropped the real median from 11.5 to 3.5, which remains the cleanest published quantification of why in-hand policies are brittle.

Eight years later, [DexReMoE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.01695) reports 19.5 average consecutive successes across 150 out-of-distribution objects in the hardest downward-facing-hand setting, with worst-case object improving from 0.69 to 6.05, but reports no real-robot transfer number. [Visual Dexterity](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/scirobotics.adc9244) (Science Robotics, peer reviewed) dropped an unseen duck-shaped object in 56% of trials, hit within 0.4 radians 75% of the time when it did not drop, and took a median 7 seconds per reorientation, implying a ceiling near 8 or 9 reorientations per minute on sub-5,000-dollar hardware.

[Bi-DexHands](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08686) (NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks) runs over 30,000 FPS on a single RTX 3090, and its authors state that existing algorithms fail at multi-task and few-shot transfer even where single-task RL succeeds. [DexVerse](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08751) (2026) spans 100 tasks but benchmarked only 19 of them, and a third-party review flags its 34% success ceiling as under-evidenced for exactly that reason. [POMDAR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.09294) (April 2026) is notable as evidence the field has only just begun defining dexterity as throughput, meaning correctness times execution speed, rather than binary success.

[DexMimicGen](https://dexmimicgen.github.io/) turned 60 human demos into over 20,000 generated demos across 9 bimanual simulation tasks, and the degradation is monotonic in contact precision: can sorting 97.3%, box cleanup 92.0%, tray lift 88.7%, transport 83.3%, piece assembly 80.7%, pouring 79.3%, coffee 77.3%, drawer cleanup 76.0%, threading 69.3%.

### Deformables: why they are structurally harder

[GarmentLab](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.01200) (NeurIPS 2024, peer reviewed), 20 tasks across 11 garment categories: the best vision method reaches 57 to 66 percent in simulation across fold, unfold and hang, while reinforcement learning collapses to 6.5 to 19.7 percent state-based and 3.2 to 15.2 percent vision-based. Real-world T-shirt folding was 10 of 15, and hat hanging 10 of 15. [Cloth Funnels](https://clothfunnels.cs.columbia.edu/) (ICRA, peer reviewed) reached up to 81.3% on real hardware zero-shot from simulation. Later garment smoothing work reports 91.1% coverage and 79.3% IoU, and the metric shift from binary success to coverage is how partial failure gets absorbed.

[DaXBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13066) (ICLR 2023) is the most useful because it publishes an expert oracle alongside the learned method, exposing the true gap: fold cloth 0.80 versus 0.91 oracle, fold T-shirt 0.59 versus 0.85, unfold cloth 0.58 versus 0.87, push rope 0.76 versus 0.93, whip rope 0.70 versus 1.00, pour water 0.58 versus 0.91, pour soup 0.42 versus 0.85. Transporter, a strong rigid-object method, craters to 0.19 on fold cloth. [SoftGym](https://sites.google.com/view/softgym) (CoRL 2020) documents that state-based algorithms still beat image-based methods on most deformable tasks because of high intrinsic dimensionality and partial observability.

The structural reasons are quantified in a few places. Deformables have effectively infinite-dimensional configuration space, so everyone discretizes to a mesh or chain of nodes ([survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.10419v1)). They lack a canonical pose and lack clearly defined end states, so the question "what does it mean for cloth to be folded" breaks both reward definition and success measurement ([2026 perspective paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.22998v1)). Simulation cost is severe: DaXBench models rope and liquid as MPM particles on a 128-cubed grid of roughly 2.1M cells, and only makes it tractable by restricting updates to an active region of 32 by 6 by 32, roughly 0.3% of the grid, which alone cuts computation by two orders of magnitude; even then one forward and backward iteration of 128 rollouts by 80 timesteps takes 3 seconds on four RTX 2080-Ti cards, with gradient checkpointing required because per-timestep memory is enormous relative to the rigid-body case.

One genuine gap in the literature: non-Markovian dynamics in deformable manipulation is widely asserted but I found no paper quantifying it with history-length ablations. The closest proxy is Dactyl's memory ablation, where the LSTM hidden state after 5 seconds of interaction predicted whether the block was larger or smaller than average in 80% of cases, showing the policy performs online system identification because a single frame is insufficient.

### Independent skepticism, which is the part most people skip

[Epoch AI, Where Autonomy Works: Evaluating Robot Capabilities in 2026](https://epoch.ai/publications/where-autonomy-works-evaluating-robot-capabilities-in-2026) is the best independent aggregator. Headline: robots are typically 3 to 10 times slower than humans. Package sorting 4 times slower, towel folding 5 times slower, shoe lacing 4 to 5 times slower, key insertion 5 times slower, trash removal 10 times slower, sandwich making 2.5 times slower, warehouse picking roughly at parity. Physical Intelligence laundry specifics: t-shirts and shorts 95%, jeans 75%, with significant degradation on inside-out clothing. ByteDance GR-RL shoe lacing 83%. Galaxea R1 bed-making instruction accuracy 78%. Physical Intelligence high-precision tasks such as key and AirPod insertion average roughly 52%.

The important counter-example is that deployment-grade reliability does exist, but only in a narrow domain with a fixed fixture: DYNA reports towel folding at 99.4% across 850-plus napkins and 99% quality acceptance across 200,000-plus towels at Monster Laundry, at roughly 40 shirts per hour, about 60% of human speed. One fabric class, one fixture, real reliability.

Quotable methodological critiques from the same source: "a task that looks solved in a demonstration may be brittle in deployment"; "most demonstrations show robots fine-tuned on specific tasks in specific environments, and unless transfer is explicitly shown it should not be assumed"; "reviewers should not infer out-of-distribution robustness from demos unless explicitly tested." The industry norm is short, heavily edited clips, and Figure's one-hour uncut sorting video is called out as notable precisely because it is the exception. Epoch also notes Tesla's Optimus trash task shows no evidence of transfer to real environments, and that Berkeley IKEA assembly used purpose-built handles, implying a grasping workaround.

Disclosure practice is uneven. Tesla's October 2024 We, Robot event used primarily teleoperated Optimus units without disclosing it. [1X](https://www.1x.tech/neo) is the clean positive case because it does disclose: NEO ships with basic autonomy and "for complex tasks NEO doesn't know, an Expert from 1X can remotely supervise its actions." Figure's BMW claims were independently contradicted: Adcock described "a fleet of robots performing end-to-end operations," while [BMW told Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/04/06/figure-ai-bmw-humanoid-robot-partnership-details-reality-exaggeration/) it was one robot, non-production hours, one pick-and-place task, later three robots for technical evaluation, with a second robot in Figure's own video standing idle and BMW declining to say whether it was functional. [TIME visited unannounced](https://time.com/7324233/figure-03-robot-humanoid-reveal/) and watched the flagship towel demo fail more often than not, one week after a clean five-towel video shipped. [Gizmodo's CES 2026 field report](https://gizmodo.com/humanoid-robots-are-here-and-embarrassingly-bad-at-being-our-servants-ces-2026-2000709300) records that when robots "weren't being controlled remotely or working off choreographed moves, these robots flailed, fell down, and broke entirely on their own."

Rodney Brooks argues dexterity will not be learned without touch, noting a human hand has roughly 17,000 low-threshold mechanoreceptors across 15 families with about 1,000 per fingertip, against vision-only pipelines delivering 1 to 3 cm precision. He predicts more than ten years to the first profitable humanoid deployment even with minimal dexterity, and that winning form factors in fifteen years will have wheels rather than feet ([source](https://rodneybrooks.com/why-todays-humanoids-wont-learn-dexterity/)). This is expert opinion, not peer-reviewed.

### Coverage gaps in this pass

Dyson household manipulation could not be sourced quantitatively; the newsroom returned HTTP 403 and no Dyson paper publishes per-task numbers comparable to Physical Intelligence, DeepMind or TRI. Treat any Dyson figure as unsourced until a primary document is found. Dedicated food-manipulation benchmarks are under-covered for the same budget reason.

### B. Where the evaluation thesis is corroborated, and where it is undermined

#### Are there buyers who would pay, or only buyers who ought to want it?

Mostly the latter, but not entirely, and the exceptions are specific enough to act on.

The strongest corroboration in all three files is not a market number, it is a spending record. Toyota Research Institute ran [1,800 real-world rollouts and over 47,000 simulation rollouts across 29 tasks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05331), with sequential hypothesis testing and blind A/B protocol, and published the conclusion that their own confidence intervals were 20 to 30 points wide and that the field may be measuring noise. That is a well-resourced industrial lab that already bought the robot-hours and received an uninterpretable answer. Physical Intelligence is doing the same thing at a smaller trial count with [RECAP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.14759). These are people already spending money badly on the problem, which is the exact standard the September 2026 postmortem established.

The second corroboration is contractual rather than technical. Performance-based RaaS contracting with 95 percent uptime SLAs at 1,500 to 8,000 dollars per unit per month is a live commercial instrument written on a measurement assumption that is false. Money is already changing hands under that clause. Insurers pricing conservatively are likewise already paying, in foregone premium and excess reserves, for the absence of a validated risk model. Both are structural, not aspirational.

Now the adversarial case, which is stronger than the September 7 session credited.

First, and most damaging: the field's revealed preference is that evaluation infrastructure is published, not purchased. TRI built lbm_eval and open-sourced 49 tasks. Berkeley built [RoboArena](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.18123) and open-sourced it with a credit economy. [Bi-DexHands](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08686), [DaXBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13066), [GarmentLab](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.01200) and [SoftGym](https://sites.google.com/view/softgym) all did the same. Every single time a sophisticated actor has had this pain, they built the tool internally and gave it away. That is not a market, it is a commons, and the burden of proof is on anyone claiming the next instance will be different.

Second, the NHTSA datum cuts the opposite way from how it was read. Withdrawing the AV STEP third-party evaluation programme in favour of case-by-case exemptions means the regulator declined to create a market for third-party evaluators. Each applicant now builds and defends its own safety case with its own lawyers and engineers. That is a services engagement, not a product, and services do not scale on a two-person team.

Third, H6's own caveat is the binding one. Every named payer class is either pre-revenue or a research lab. Robotics raised 18.8 billion dollars in 2026 with humanoids taking 8.6 billion of it, and the same market document rates humanoid labour WEAK with no disclosed revenue anywhere. Selling measurement to that cohort means revenue is a derivative of their funding cycle. TUV and UL already hold the auditor brand and the regulatory relationships, so the natural exit is absorption, not victory.

Fourth, and this is the argument I would lead with if I were trying to kill the thesis: the measurement problem may be self-liquidating. The one deployment-grade datum in the evidence file is DYNA at 99.4 percent towel folding across 850-plus napkins and 99 percent quality acceptance across 200,000-plus towels. Notice what that implies. When reliability is real, the trial counts arrive free, because the robot runs 200,000 times in production. Statistically valid evaluation is cheap exactly where the business works and expensive exactly where it does not. Whoever reaches deployment gets their statistics from telemetry at zero marginal cost.

The defensible residue after that argument is narrow but real. Production telemetry tells you about the deployed policy on the deployed distribution. It cannot tell you whether an over-the-air update is a regression, because you cannot run the previous policy counterfactually in production without surrendering throughput. So the measurement problem at the moment of model update does not self-liquidate. That, and not general evaluation, is the surviving thesis, and it is meaningfully smaller than the September 7 framing.

#### Does IRIS already own this thrust?

No, and the roster is unambiguous about it. Evaluation, verification and benchmarking is thin inside IRIS despite a healthy paper count, because IRIS members are middle authors: RoboArena is led from Berkeley, the verification papers belong to Jacky Kwok with Pavone and Mirhoseini, RoboMME comes from Michigan. Only Yuejiang Liu owns any of it and he departs for NUS in 2027. That is a genuine thin spot, and it is thin in the specific way that favours a newcomer: the lab has appetite for the topic, demonstrated by six papers, and no internal claimant.

RoboArena cuts both ways and the direction matters.

Against: it establishes a canonical, open-source, seven-university answer to "how do we compare generalist policies," it carries IRIS alumnus Karl Pertsch's name, and it means any pitch now opens with "how is this not RoboArena." It also means Finn has already watched an evaluation paper emerge from her orbit and be led from Berkeley, which lowers the novelty premium on the topic.

For, and I judge this the stronger read: RoboArena is a protocol and a platform, not a statistical theory. It reports [612 pairwise comparisons across 7 policies at 7 universities](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.18123), which is roughly 29 comparisons per policy pair if spread uniformly. Its central claim is more accurate ranking at retained sample efficiency, and it publishes no estimator variance, no evaluator random-effect model, and no treatment of the fact that evaluators freely choose their own tasks and scenes. Self-selected tasks is textbook non-ignorable missingness. Bradley-Terry on 29 noisy comparisons per pair with heterogeneous raters and self-selected items has a confidence interval that nobody has computed. Further, the credit economy exists precisely because robot-hours are the scarce resource, which concedes the premise. RoboArena is not the competitor. It is the substrate, the dataset and the open problem, and it comes with users already attached.

#### Net judgement

The thesis splits. As a research direction it is strong: thin inside IRIS, unclaimed, substrate available, and an exact match to a signal-processing background where adaptive sampling and estimator design are the native skills. As a business it is CONTESTED at best, because the commons argument, the self-liquidation argument and the pre-revenue-customer argument each independently damage it, and none has been tested against a real buyer.

### C. Project proposals, ranked

#### P1. Non-regression certification for over-the-air policy updates

**Question.** Given a deployed policy and a candidate update, what is the cheapest sequential test certifying non-regression at stated confidence? Specifically: does blocking on identical initial conditions, with common random numbers and a shared failure-mode prior across successive checkpoints, break below the one-over-delta-squared independent-sampling bound?

**Why it matters.** The 1,570-rollouts-per-arm figure assumes unpaired independent Bernoulli sampling. That assumption is wrong for consecutive checkpoints of the same model, which share failure modes. A demonstrated threefold variance reduction moves 1,570 to roughly 520, which is the boundary between infeasible and routine.

**Attachment.** Lars Ankile, because his real-hardware residual-RL claims live entirely in the small-trial regime, and secondarily Marcel Torne for the failure definition. Ankile has no thrust competitor and is one year ahead, not five.

**Method.** Paired Bernoulli and McNemar-type tests, common-random-number variance reduction, stratified and blocked designs, sequential probability ratio tests with alpha spending, and a learned critic used as a control variate, which is where Van Roy's MDP course and doubly robust off-policy evaluation enter.

**Access.** Simulation-led on lbm_eval's 49 tasks plus LIBERO and RoboMimic, with a small real check on a Franka. This is exactly the simulation-led-with-real-check pattern Yuejiang Liu already runs, so it is realistic for an MS student.

**First falsifiable experiment.** Take two published checkpoints differing by roughly 5 points, run paired against unpaired evaluation in simulation, measure realised variance reduction. Six to eight weeks. Falsified if pairing buys under 1.5x, which would mean policy stochasticity dominates initial-condition variance and the entire variance-reduction route is dead.

**Buyer.** RaaS operators and their insurers, who would pay for a trial schedule and a signed non-regression report attached to an SLA renewal.

**Strongest case against.** The dominant variance may live in the task distribution rather than the initial condition, and deployment nonstationarity breaks the pairing assumption exactly when it is needed. And if it works, it may be a twenty-page appendix in someone else's paper rather than a company.

#### P2. What counts as a failure: elicited failure taxonomy for contact-rich tasks

**Question.** Can a failure definition for contact-rich and deformable tasks be elicited from operators at inter-rater reliability above a stated threshold, and does a graded failure metric reduce evaluation variance against binary success at fixed trial count?

**Why it matters.** This is the precondition for everything else, and the evidence file shows the field is already quietly substituting metrics to conceal partial failure. [Pi-zero-point-five](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.16054v1) reports task progress rather than success. Garment smoothing reports coverage and IoU. [POMDAR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.09294) redefines dexterity as throughput. Each substitution is a new estimator and not one is validated against the quantity a buyer cares about.

**Attachment.** Marcel Torne, whose Freeform Preference Learning already did the elicitation half with two MS students as co-authors, plus Yuejiang Liu on state plausibility as a checkable predicate.

**Method.** Krippendorff alpha, item response theory to calibrate rater severity, graded response models, variance decomposition of binary against graded metrics at fixed n, and a validation step showing the graded metric predicts a downstream production outcome.

**Access.** Human annotation over existing rollout video from DROID, Open X-Embodiment and lbm_eval. Zero robot hours. The most accessible project in the set and startable before matriculation.

**First falsifiable experiment.** Two hundred rollout videos of one deformable task, five annotators, compare binary-success agreement against graded agreement. Three to five weeks and roughly a thousand dollars of annotation. Falsified if binary agreement already exceeds alpha of 0.8, in which case the definitional crisis is legal rather than statistical.

**Buyer.** SLA drafters on both sides, and the ISO 25785-1 working group. Standards-adjacent definitional work is how small firms get written into procurement language, which is the most durable moat on offer here.

**Strongest case against.** It is psychometrics with robots attached, and nobody at IRIS can evaluate psychometrics, which means no internal champion and a hard venue story. The worst plausible outcome is that the definition is adopted for free and the author captures none of it.

#### P3. Estimator audit of pairwise policy arenas

**Question.** What is the sampling distribution of a Bradley-Terry policy ranking when evaluators self-select tasks and scenes, and how many comparisons per pair certify a ranking? Does an evaluator random-intercept model with inverse-propensity weighting on task selection change the published RoboArena ordering?

**Attachment.** Yuejiang Liu, whose thesis is that verification is cheaper than prediction. This quantifies "cheaper." It also makes IRIS the statistical authority over an artifact Berkeley owns the platform for.

**Method.** Bradley-Terry with random evaluator and task effects, group sequential testing with O'Brien-Fleming boundaries, selection models for non-ignorable missingness, and minimum-detectable-effect curves. Choosing which pair to evaluate next is adaptive experimental design, which is the student's published TSP expertise transplanted directly. This is the tightest skill match in the set.

**Access.** Released RoboArena comparison data plus lbm_eval. Zero robot hours.

**First falsifiable experiment.** Refit the 612 comparisons with evaluator and task random effects and test whether the published top-two ordering is statistically distinguishable. Two to four weeks. Falsified if the ranking is robust and the intervals are already tight.

**Buyer.** Anyone signing a 95 percent SLA, and the arena's own credit currency.

**Strongest case against.** It is a reanalysis paper. Reanalyses place badly, read as an attack on a friendly lab, and would burn Karl Pertsch, who is the best alumni bridge available. No robot appears anywhere in it, which in a robotics lab reads as not robotics.

#### P4. Calibrated surrogate evaluation: the exchange rate between simulated and real rollouts

**Question.** Can a learned verifier or simulator be calibrated to carry a certified real-world coverage guarantee, and what is the exchange rate in real rollouts saved per surrogate rollout?

**Why it matters.** The market document could not verify any sim-to-real correlation coefficient. [Dactyl](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.00177) shows why: simulation median 50 consecutive rotations against real median 13, vision-based 33 against 11.5, and withholding observation-noise randomisation alone dropped the real median from 11.5 to 3.5. A surrogate with a fourfold bias that swings on one randomisation choice is worthless as a point predictor and potentially excellent as a control variate. That reframing is the contribution.

**Method.** Conformal prediction for distribution-free coverage on the real-versus-surrogate residual, prediction-powered inference to combine a small real sample with a large surrogate sample under a rectifier term, control variates, doubly robust off-policy evaluation.

**Access.** lbm_eval's 49 simulation tasks paired against TRI's 1,800 published real rollouts is close to an ideal paired dataset for exactly this, plus DROID.

**First falsifiable experiment.** Fit a prediction-powered-inference estimator on lbm_eval simulation scores plus a subsample of real rollouts and measure the effective-sample-size multiplier. Four to six weeks. Falsified below roughly 1.3x.

**Buyer.** Anyone running a simulation farm, and NVIDIA specifically, given Cosmos Policy is an existing IRIS-NVIDIA channel. What they buy is a CFO-legible justification of the simulation budget.

**Strongest case against.** Exchangeability fails exactly when the policy or fixture changes, which is exactly when you need the guarantee, so coverage would be technically valid and practically vacuous. Prediction-powered inference is also a crowded statistics area and someone will do the robotics application regardless.

#### P5. Adaptive rollout allocation under a robot-hour budget

Best-arm identification and D-optimal design for allocating a fixed robot-hour budget across K policies and T tasks. It is the cleanest possible match to the student's adaptive non-uniform sampling publication and to Van Roy's course, and it can produce a first result in two to three weeks from pure simulation. It ranks last because nobody at IRIS owns it, so there is no champion; because bandit venues will call the setting unremarkable while robotics venues call the method standard; and because the honest answer may be "use successive halving," which is a blog post rather than a paper.

**Ranking: P1, P2, P3, P4, P5.** P1 carries the best combination of commercial legibility and an uncrowded, early-career internal champion. P2 is the most accessible and is a precondition for the rest, with the weakest venue story. P3 is the sharpest publishable hole and the most politically awkward. P4 has the best methodology and assumptions that break where it counts. P5 is the best skill fit and the worst positioning.

### D. Risk register

**The Physical Intelligence conflict is the largest risk and it is structural, not procedural.** Chelsea Finn co-founded Physical Intelligence, and the roster shows PI is the lab's dominant industry channel: Lucy Shi has been a core member since March 2024, Torne spent nine months there, Swerdlow interned, and Sridhar's MemER is built on pi-zero-point-five. Now consider what an honest measurement programme produces as output. The sharpest findings available from the evidence file are that pi-zero-point-five substituted task progress for success rate, and that RECAP's halved-failure-rate claim is underpowered by its own 600-trajectory budget. Both are true and both are adverse to the advisor's company. An independent evaluator of robot policies is structurally adversarial to policy vendors. This is not a disclosure problem, it is a thesis-selection problem: the student should expect to be steered toward evaluation methods that help the lab train better and away from evaluation methods that audit vendor claims, and the audit version is the commercial version. If the real plan is an audit business, IRIS under Finn is a conflicted home and REALab under Shuran Song is materially cleaner on this one axis. That should weigh on the lab choice more than research fit does.

**Stanford IP and OTL.** Inventions made with material university resources or under sponsored research are disclosable and Stanford's default posture is ownership. The specific hazard here is that the asset is a method plus a calibration dataset rather than code, and sponsored agreements with industrial partners can carry background-IP and publication-review terms a student never sees. Concrete mitigations: obtain the sponsored-research terms for whatever project is joined before joining it, keep all falsification work and any customer-facing artifact off Stanford compute and outside any sponsored project, and take this to OTL as a conversation rather than guessing. MS students are typically not on sponsored funding, which is protective but not dispositive.

**Publishing against secrecy, which is worse here than in most theses.** A measurement method's entire value is credibility, and credibility requires publication, because the buyer's counterparty will not accept an unauditable method. The method must therefore be public and the moat must be elsewhere: the calibration dataset, the auditor relationship, a standards-committee seat, or an operating record. Any plan that treats the method as the moat is wrong. The field has priced evaluation tooling at zero every single time.

**Master's latitude is limited and the roster proves it.** Of eight IRIS MS students, Armaan Abraham is first author on one paper with Lucy Shi and Finn, Ke Wang is co-first with a postdoc, and Mahajan and Bhat are second and third authors on Torne's paper. Three have no findable record and one is unconfirmed. So roughly half of IRIS MS students produce a visible paper, and the modal path is second or third author inside a senior member's agenda. Abraham is the exception and note that even he did it inside an existing thrust. The realistic read is two to three quarters executing a defined slice of someone else's question before earning the right to propose, against a total programme of roughly six quarters. One first-author paper is a realistic ceiling; two is not. This is the single best argument for P2, whose first experiment completes in three to five weeks.

**Repeating the borrowed-pain failure through the co-founder's network is the highest-probability repeat of a known error.** The structural feature that produced nine double-counted LOIs is still present: the co-founder's asset is relationships, and relationships are the thing that manufactures false demand signal. In this market the trap has a precise shape. Warm introductions lead to robotics founders and venture investors, because they take meetings and enjoy talking about evaluation. A robotics founder saying evaluation is a huge problem is worth nothing, because they are pre-revenue, they will build it in-house, and the field's entire history says they will then open-source it. That is a warm, enthusiastic, free and completely false positive. Rule: no letter of intent counts, and no conversation counts unless the person names a dollar figure already spent or already lost. Second rule: the co-founder does not source the first ten conversations, because the prior failure is direct evidence that this sourcing channel yields contaminated samples.

**Co-founder fit is a real problem and should be named rather than managed.** Freight logistics relationships do not transfer. The buyers here are reliability engineers, actuaries, standards committee members and VPs of deployment. None is reachable through freight brokerage, and none buys on relationship: actuaries buy on model documentation, committees buy on technical contribution, reliability engineers buy on a reproducible benchmark. Worse, the co-founder's product instinct was calibrated on software sold to back-office buyers on a short cycle, whereas this is a long, credibility-gated cycle where the technical founder's publication record is the sales asset. That inverts the division of labour the pair currently assumes. The honest options are that the co-founder retools into robotics deployment operations over about a year with no guarantee, that the pair choose a market where freight relationships genuinely are the asset and abandon the September 7 direction, or that they separate. One narrow role does survive: the co-founder can run the falsification interviews below, because interviewing transfers even though the network does not.

**Four further risks, stated briefly.** The thesis is a derivative of a funding bubble, with 18.8 billion dollars raised in 2026 and humanoids at 46 percent of it while the humanoid hypothesis is itself rated WEAK. TUV and UL may absorb the function. Yuejiang Liu departs within roughly one year of matriculation, removing the only natural mentor, while Finn's attention is split across 33 people and a company. And the direction was selected in a strategy session held days after winding down a failed startup, which is a decision made in a recoil state and deserves one cold re-litigation before it becomes a career.

### E. Falsification plan: three tests, 60 days, no lab access

Pre-register the binary before the first call: proceed only if at least two of the three tests return a named individual who can quantify money already spent or already lost on this problem. Enthusiasm, agreement and introductions do not count.

**Test 1, the SLA penalty interview. Run this first because it is cheapest and most likely to kill.** Target the person who administers an uptime clause, not a founder. Entry points: GXO, which holds an Agility Robotics RaaS contract and therefore has a contract administrator and a site operations lead on the receiving end; and the third-party logistics operators buying from Locus, Geek+ and Hai Robotics. Cold-source by 3PL operations job title, never through a robotics investor. Ask: walk me through the last time you applied or contested an uptime credit, who measured and what did you argue about; when your vendor pushed a software update, what did you require before accepting it and what did that cost; what did you spend last year verifying vendor performance claims, in headcount or in credits surrendered. **Kill condition:** if three or more operators say the SLA is measured on robot availability rather than task success and nobody disputes task quality, the unenforceable-SLA premise is wrong, because the contract was written around the measurement problem rather than into it. This is the most likely way the thesis dies and it is knowable in three weeks.

**Test 2, the internal-eval-team audit. This probes build-versus-buy, which is the thesis's true weak point.** Target engineers already paid to build an evaluation harness: the lbm_eval authors at Toyota Research Institute, the RoboArena authors via Karl Pertsch but asking about cost rather than collaboration, and above all the reliability and evaluation engineers at companies actually shipping, meaning Symbotic, Amazon Robotics, Dexterity, Ambi Robotics and DYNA. DYNA is the single most valuable conversation available, because they are the only entity in the evidence file with deployment-grade statistics and will know exactly what those cost. Ask: how many engineer-months went into your evaluation infrastructure and who owns it now; how many robot-hours per month go to evaluation rather than production; what decision do you currently make on evidence you know is insufficient; and the discriminating question, would you ever accept a third party's number in place of your own. **Kill condition:** if the uniform answer is six engineer-months, good enough, and never an external number, then the business is dead while the research remains fine, and the correct conclusion is to do the research and not the startup.

**Test 3, the insurance underwriting interview. Run last, because these schedule slowly.** Target specialty-lines underwriters and actuaries at carriers already writing robotics and AV risk, plus the brokers placing it. Ask: what data does a robotics applicant give you today and what do you do with it; what is your current loading for model-update uncertainty and how was that number chosen; if an applicant handed you a validated failure-rate estimate with a confidence interval, would your rate change and who internally must approve that. The last clause is the real test, because rate-model changes require actuarial filing, which is institutionally slow. **Kill condition:** if underwriters price robotics off general and product liability templates with a judgemental load and no appetite to change the model, the insurance buyer is a decade out and one of only two named payer classes disappears. The asymmetry is worth noting: a single carrier willing to build a programme around a validated model is a design partner with a budget, which is the best available outcome from all three tests combined.

## 5. Closing: What You Now Know, What to Study Next, and My Recommendations

### What you now know

IRIS has 33 current members excluding Chelsea Finn, comprising 2 postdocs, 13 PhD students, 2 rotating and 2 visiting PhD students, 8 MS students, 1 visiting MS student and 5 undergraduates, and the lab's thrusts are unevenly staffed in ways that matter more than the headcount. Reinforcement learning for expressive policies is the most crowded and most single-person dominated thrust, with Perry Dong first-authoring eight of roughly eleven papers, so entering there means competing with the lab's most productive author on his own topic. Generalist policies and vision-language-action models is crowded by headcount and gated by resources, since several of those papers are effectively Physical Intelligence or NVIDIA collaborations with privileged data access. Language-model post-training is crowded today but scheduled to empty, because Yoonho Lee and Anikait Singh are both final-year. Against that, four thrusts are thin: memory and long-horizon control, held by Marcel Torne and Ajay Sridhar who are both mid-PhD and therefore growing rather than vacating; world models, whose flagship papers are co-first-authored by an alumnus and whose only current owner leaves for the National University of Singapore in 2027; surgical robotics, which has the lab's highest-prestige placement in Science Robotics and no permanent occupant; and dexterous contact-rich manipulation, which is essentially Lars Ankile alone, admitted Autumn 2025. Two thrusts are effectively vacant, robustness and distribution shift having emptied when Yoonho Lee pivoted, and theory having left with its alumni. Dorsa Sadigh functions as a second principal investigator for the reinforcement-learning half of the lab, formally co-advising five students, which means "joining IRIS" often means joining a Finn and Sadigh project.

On the market side, robotics has never been limited by manipulation cleverness. It has been limited by the cost of specifying a task precisely enough to repeat it without a human in the loop, and every one of the seventeen sectors at commercial scale solved that by changing the environment rather than by making the robot smarter: fixtures in automotive, tape-and-reel in electronics, the tote in warehousing, the ISO container in ports, the SBS microplate in biology, the geofence and high-definition map in robotaxis. Dairy milking is the instructive exception, the one genuinely deformable biological manipulation task that shipped at scale, and it shipped because the search space was reduced to four targets in a fixed frame with a cooperative workpiece that presents itself. Of eight hypotheses tested against evidence, three came back strong, two contested and three weak. Certification and evaluation infrastructure for learned controllers is strong and matches your background. Collapsing integration and teaching cost is strong with Rethink Robotics' two separate failures sitting directly on it, the lesson being that usability without precision does not sell. Geofenced autonomy is strong as a validated pattern but demanded 27 billion dollars of capital at Waymo and destroyed Cruise at over 10 billion. Arbitrary-SKU piece picking and deformable food and meat cutting are contested, with the latter the most underattacked gap relative to its value. General-purpose humanoid labour, restaurant automation and physical elder care are weak, the last despite being the largest labour shortfall in the table at 4,677,100 jobs and roughly 760,500 annual openings, because willingness to pay is intermediated by payers who do not pay.

You also now know the capability ceiling in numbers rather than demonstrations, which is the part the field obscures. The same model with the same hand unscrews a lightbulb 92 percent of the time and screws it back in 36 percent. Trash bag tying is 44 percent, ziplock 40 percent and dustpan 32 percent. Physical Intelligence's own model card concedes that before pi-zero-point-six, laundry folding and box assembly had zero out-of-box success without task-specific fine-tuning, and that pi-zero-point-six fully assembles the box 20 percent of the time. Robots are typically 3 to 10 times slower than humans at the tasks they can do. The sim-to-real gap has barely closed in eight years: Dactyl achieved a median of 50 consecutive rotations in simulation against 13 on hardware after roughly 100 simulated years of experience, and withholding observation-noise randomization alone dropped the real median from 11.5 to 3.5. Figure told investors it had a fleet performing end-to-end operations at BMW while BMW described one robot, off-hours, on one pick-and-place task, and TIME watched the flagship towel demo fail more often than not one week after a clean video shipped. Finally, and most usefully for you, Toyota Research Institute published that at 50 rollouts their confidence intervals were 20 to 30 points wide and that the field may be measuring noise, which is an insider admission that the standard protocol cannot support the standard claim.

- [ ] Read the TRI [careful examination](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05331) and reproduce their confidence-interval claim yourself from the reported trial counts, so you can quote it from arithmetic rather than from authority.

### My recommendations

**Separate the research decision from the company decision, and take only the first one now.** The research case is strong on evidence from two independent directions, and you should stop reopening it; the business case is contested and has not been tested against a single real buyer. These are not the same bet and conflating them is how you end up building a company to justify a research interest. Join the lab, do the work, and let the three interview tests decide the company question on evidence. Nothing about doing excellent measurement research forecloses starting the company in eighteen months, whereas committing to the company now forecloses hearing a negative answer.

**Pick the failure-definition project first, even though it is the least sophisticated of the five.** It completes a first falsifiable experiment in three to five weeks on existing rollout video with zero robot hours and roughly a thousand dollars of annotation, it attaches to Marcel Torne whose thrust already has two MS students as co-authors and is therefore demonstrated MS-accessible, and it is logically prior to everything else, because you cannot build a statistically valid estimator of a quantity nobody has defined. The field is already substituting metrics to conceal partial failure, with pi-zero-point-five reporting task progress, garment work reporting coverage and IoU, and POMDAR redefining dexterity as throughput, and not one of those substitutions has been validated against the quantity a buyer actually cares about.
